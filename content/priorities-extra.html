01Mind

The Psychology of SurvivalHaving survival skills is important; having the will to survive is essential.

FM 3-05.70 opens its psychology chapter with an observation from real emergencies: some people with little or no training have survived life-threatening circumstances, and some trained people have died. The difference is rarely the kit. It is the mental attitude of the individual. Without a desire to survive, acquired skills serve little purpose and invaluable knowledge goes to waste.

Stress Is a Tool, Not a Disease

Stress is not something you cure and eliminate — it is your reaction to pressure, and you need some of it. Stress provides challenges, tests adaptability, and stimulates you to perform at your best. The goal is stress without excess. Too much leads to distress, and stressors are not courteous: they arrive in piles, and the cumulative effect of minor stressors can become a major distress if they land close together. As the body's resistance wears down, exhaustion arrives and distress appears. The survivor is the person who works with his stresses instead of letting his stresses work on him.

The fight-or-flee response

When a stressor is recognized, the body sends an internal SOS: stored fuels (sugar and fats) release for quick energy, breathing accelerates, muscles tense, blood-clotting mechanisms activate, senses sharpen — hearing sharpens, pupils dilute, smell acute — and heart rate and blood pressure rise. Useful, but it cannot be maintained indefinitely.

The six survival stressors

Injury, illness, or death; uncertainty and lack of control; the environment (weather, terrain, heat, cold, insects, dangerous animals); hunger and thirst; fatigue; and isolation. What stresses one person may not stress another — experience, training, outlook, conditioning, and self-confidence decide.

The 80-10-10 rule

The old "Art of Survival" course distilled it to percentages: survival is 80 percent attitude, 10 percent equipment, and 10 percent the skill and knowledge to use that equipment. The graveyards are full of people who said "it could never happen to me."

Signs of Distress — Watch for These in Yourself and Others
  • Decision trouble: difficulty making decisions, constant worrying, a propensity for mistakes.
  • Energy and mood: low energy level, angry outbursts, forgetfulness, carelessness, thoughts about death or suicide.
  • Social withdrawal: trouble getting along with others, withdrawing from others, hiding from responsibilities.

Natural Reactions to an Unnatural Situation

Fear, anxiety, anger, guilt, depression, and loneliness are normal reactions — "natural reactions to this unnatural situation." Controlled, they sharpen you; uncontrolled, they bring psychological defeat long before you physically succumb.

Fear

The emotional response to danger. In healthy form it encourages caution; unchecked it immobilizes you until you fail to do essential tasks. There is no shame in fear — train yourself not to be overcome by it.

Anxiety

Can urge you to act to end the dangers threatening you, and doing survival tasks reduces it. Overwhelming anxiety confuses thinking until good judgment becomes impossible.

Anger and frustration

Events you cannot control — weather, terrain, broken gear — generate impulsive reactions and poorly thought-out decisions. Harnessed, the emotional intensity can be spent productively; unfocused, it wastes energy.

Depression

Anger and frustration feed a destructive cycle until focus shifts from "what can I do" to "there is nothing I can do." Remembering loved ones can fuel the desire to live one more day; sinking into depression saps the will to survive.

Loneliness and boredom

Isolation can surface imagination, creativity, and a reservoir of inner strength you never knew you had — or become another source of depression. Keep the mind productively occupied and build self-sufficiency.

Guilt

Survivors commonly feel guilty about being spared when others were not. Used positively, it drives people to carry on the work of those killed. The living who abandon their chance to survive accomplish nothing.

Preparing Yourself Before It Happens

  • Know yourself: discover through training, family, and friends who you are on the inside; strengthen strong qualities and develop the areas you know you will need.
  • Anticipate fears: do not pretend you will have none. Train in the areas that frighten you most — the goal is not to eliminate fear but to build confidence in functioning despite it.
  • Be realistic: see circumstances as they are, not as you want them to be. Follow the adage "hope for the best, prepare for the worst" — it is easier to adjust to pleasant surprises than to harsh ones.
  • Adopt a positive attitude: learn to see the potential good in everything; it boosts morale and exercises imagination.
  • Remind yourself what is at stake: your life and the lives of others who depend on you. Failure to prepare psychologically brings carelessness, lost confidence, poor decisions, and giving up before the body gives in.
  • Train and rehearse: the more realistic the training, the less overwhelming the real setting. Practice with your kit until each item works in rain, dark, and stress.
  • Learn stress management: relaxation skills, time management, assertiveness, and cognitive restructuring — controlling how you view a situation.
The will to survive is also the refusal to give up. Value living — stubbornness in the face of problems and obstacles supplies the mental and physical strength to endure.
02Priorities

The Survival Plan and PatternFailure to plan is a plan to fail — plan, prepare, practice.

A survival plan stands on three intertwined parts: planning (realizing something could happen and taking steps now), preparation (readying yourself and your kit for the contingencies in the plan), and practice (checking that items work and that you can use them). A plan without preparation is just a piece of paper; it will not keep you alive. Plans are built on evasion and recovery considerations: mission duration, distance to friendly lines, terrain, weather and its possible changes, and your platform — aircraft, vehicle, or rucksack. Memorize the major geographical features along your routes in case map and compass are lost.

The Keyword: SURVIVAL

FM 21-76 is built entirely on the keyword SURVIVAL — each letter a rule of action for the first minutes and hours.

The Pattern of Survival

Develop a survival pattern that beats the enemies of survival: food, water, shelter, fire, first aid, and signals, placed in order of importance for your situation. In a cold environment the order might run fire (warmth), shelter (protection from wind and wet), traps and snares (food), a signaling means, and first aid to maintain health. If injured, first aid has top priority in any climate. Change the pattern as the environment changes — the pattern serves your immediate physical needs, not a fixed list. The wilderness handbooks add a second framing: the Big Three — shelter, fire, and signal — with shelter the number-one priority of life, because with adequate shelter you are probably still alive when rescuers come.

The Seven Enemies

Fear is generally followed by panic, then these follow one another in a descending spiral; calmly assess the situation and do not let them interfere.

  • Pain: may be ignored in panic — deal with injuries immediately before they worsen.
  • Cold: lowers the ability to think and reduces the will to survive; never stop moving or sleep unless adequately sheltered.
  • Thirst: dulls the mind and makes you overlook important information.
  • Hunger: seldom deadly, but reduces logical thought and increases susceptibility to cold, pain, and fear.
  • Fatigue: unavoidable and lowers mental ability — often the body's way of escaping a difficult situation.
  • Boredom and loneliness: unanticipated enemies that lower the mind's ability to cope.
Layer the Kit — Body, Vest, Platform
  • On your body: the most important items — map and compass, knife, lighter, basic life-sustaining gear. If you lose everything else, these survive with you.
  • On load-bearing equipment: backups and second-line items — pen flares behind the pocket signal mirror, a magnesium bar behind the pocket lighter.
  • On the platform: bulky items ride in the rucksack, vehicle, or aircraft. Items should be multipurpose, compact, lightweight, durable, and functional.
  • Seven kit categories: water, fire, shelter, food, medical, signal, miscellaneous — each stocked to sustain that basic need.
  • The case: water-repellent or waterproof, easy to carry or attach, able to hold various sizes, durable. Print instructions for medical items so stress cannot cause a life-threatening error.
03Evasion

Camouflage and Evasion MovementStay out of sight — a moving object is easy to spot.

The multiservice Survival, Evasion, and Recovery manual (FM 21-76-1) reduces evasion to guidelines: keep a positive attitude, use established procedures, follow your evasion plan of action, be patient, drink water (do not eat food without water), conserve strength for critical periods, rest and sleep as much as possible, and stay out of sight. Your immediate actions after isolation: think before you act, protect yourself from nuclear, biological, or chemical hazards, seek a concealed site, assess and treat medical condition, sanitize the uniform of compromising information, hide equipment you are leaving, apply personal camouflage, then move away from the concealed site — a zigzag pattern is recommended — using terrain for advantage, and find a hole-up site.

Personal Camouflage

Choose the pattern

Blotch pattern for temperate deciduous (leaf-shedding) areas, barren desert, and barren snow. Slash pattern with broad slashes for coniferous and jungle areas, narrow slashes for grass. The two may be combined.

Paint the face

Dark colors on the high spots — forehead, cheekbones, nose, chin — and light colors on remaining exposed areas. The insides and backs of the ears take two colors to break up outlines. Cover head, neck, hands, and under the chin with scarf, collar, vegetation, or netting.

Break the shape

The goal is to break up the outline of the body, including the "V" of the crotch and armpits. Give light-colored hair special attention under a scarf or mosquito head net. Remove unit patches, name tags, and rank insignia.

  • Disturb as little as possible: avoid activity that reveals movement, and avoid unnecessary movement once in position.
  • Change with the surroundings: cut foliage fades and wilts — change it regularly, never take all vegetation from the same source, and use stains from grasses, berries, dirt, and charcoal.
  • Do not over camouflage: excess texture looks as wrong as bare skin, and remember shadows shift with the sun.
  • Never expose shiny objects: watch, glasses, pens. Turn off watch alarms and hourly chimes.
  • Observe prone: conduct observation from a prone and concealed position, never from a skyline.
  • Odors betray you: scented soaps and shampoos, shaving cream and after-shave, insect repellent (camouflage stick is the least scented form), gum and candy, and tobacco — its odor is unmistakable.
Shelter Sites: BLISS
  • B — Blend with the surroundings; the site should need minimal to no preparation.
  • L — Low silhouette; keep the shelter small and hard to see.
  • I — Irregular shape; geometry draws the eye, lumps do not.
  • S — Small; just large enough for the occupants.
  • S — Secluded location: drainages and rough terrain are least likely to be searched; ensure overhead concealment, multiple escape routes (never corner yourself), observable approaches, and beware flash floods in ravines and canyons.
Concealing Evidence of Travel
  • Hands off the greenery: do not break branches, leaves, or grass, do not disturb vegetation above knee level, and use a walking stick to part vegetation and push it back in place. Grabbing small trees scuffs bark and, in snow country, leaves a path of snowless vegetation marking your route.
  • Firm footing: place the foot lightly but squarely; avoid overturning ground cover, rocks, and sticks, scuffing bark, or mangling grass that should spring back. Cloth wrapped around the feet muffles breaking sticks.
  • Mask tracks: place them in the shadows of vegetation, downed logs, and snowdrifts; move before and during precipitation so tracks fill in; travel in windy periods; use solid surfaces like logs and rock; pat tracks lightly to age them.
  • Take nothing, leave nothing: hide or bury discarded items — trash or lost equipment identifies who lost it.
  • Route discipline: irregular routes and zigzags camouflage your direction; if pursued by dogs, concentrate on defeating the handler, not the dog.

Movement Rhythm: Stop, Look, Listen, Smell

  1. 1

    Stop

    At irregular intervals, halt at a point of concealment — not on a schedule an observer could predict. Restrict movement to periods of low light, bad weather, wind, or reduced enemy activity, and mask it with natural cover.

  2. 2

    Look

    For signs of human or animal activity — smoke, tracks, roads, troops, vehicles, aircraft, wire, buildings — and for trip wires and booby traps. Peripheral vision is more effective for recognizing movement at night and twilight.

  3. 3

    Listen

    For vehicles, troops, aircraft, weapons, and animals. Check clothing and equipment for items that could make noise and secure them; noise discipline is continuous.

  4. 4

    Smell

    For vehicles, troops, animals, and fires. Use the military crest of ridges rather than the skyline, avoid silhouetting on crests, and move from one concealed area to another.

Obstacles have set procedures: enter deep ditches feet first; go around chain-link and wire fences, or cross under at damaged areas without touching — look for electrical insulators or security devices; pass under or between the lower rails of rail fences, or go over presenting the lowest silhouette possible. Before crossing a road, observe from concealment, cross at bushes, shadows, or a bend, and leave footprints parallel to the road with a cross-step sideways. Cross railroad tracks lying face down, aligned parallel to the rails, using a semi-pushup motion.

Hide site discipline. In hostile areas, hide during the day and move at night. Do not occupy a hide site more than 24 hours; do not build fires or prepare food there — smoke and food odors reveal your position; restrict all movement to less than 45 centimeters above the ground; enter by a button hook or other deceptive route, conduct a listening halt first, and sterilize the site before leaving. Avoid roads, trails, inhabited areas, waterways and bridges, natural lines of drift, man-made structures, and all civilian and military personnel even when bypassing costs time and energy.
04Recovery

Signaling and RecoveryEvery transmission and signal can bring rescue — or compromise your position.

Recovery is a two-way drill: you help the recovery force find you, and you follow their instructions exactly. Establish radio contact as soon as possible or as directed in the applicable plans, maintain communication until recovered, and be prepared to authenticate. If no radio survives, a ground-to-air signal may be your only means of effecting recovery. Be ready to report enemy activity in the recovery area, the site's characteristics (slope, obstacles, size), the number in your party and their medical situation, and which signal devices you have.

Radio Discipline

  • Keep transmissions short: 3 to 5 seconds maximum, data burst if available — and in combat, move after each transmission. Use terrain masking to hinder enemy direction finding.
  • Antenna craft: keep the antenna perpendicular to the intended receiver, face the recovery asset, and never ground the antenna with a finger, attaching bolt, space blanket, or vegetation.
  • Choose the site: a concealment site that optimizes line of sight; if transmitting in the blind, ensure clear line of sight toward the equator. Consider the direction-finding threat before transmitting from shelter.
  • Batteries: locate spares early and keep them warm and dry; follow standing on/off schedules to conserve charge. In combat, turn the locator beacon off but keep it with you.

Signal mirror

Use as directed by recovery forces; with no radio, use only with confirmed friendly forces. Improvise one from any shiny metal or glass, sweep the horizon, and keep the mirror covered when not in use.

Fire and smoke

Fire at night; smoke by day — tires or petroleum products for dark smoke, green vegetation for light. Strobe and infrared lights: prepare early, consider filters and shields, conserve battery, and remember the one residual flash at turn-off.

Pattern signals

Build them large with straight lines and sharp corners, in materials that contrast in color and texture with the surroundings — space blanket, panniers, parachute, or natural rock, brush, and stomped grass. Maximize visibility from above while concealing the site from ground observation. Sea dye marker: do not waste it in rough seas, rewrap the unused portion, and it can color snow.

The Recovery Sequence

  1. 1

    Select the site

    If practical, an area about 150 feet in diameter, free of obstructions, flat and level. In combat, assess evidence of human activity at or near the site, locate several concealment sites around the area, and plan several tactical entry and exit routes.

  2. 2

    Prepare

    Pack and secure all equipment, prepare signaling devices as directed or briefed, and mentally review the recovery methods — aircraft, ground, boat, hoist — so nothing surprises you.

  3. 3

    Identify your position

    Assist the recovery force in finding you and stay concealed until recovery is imminent. Assist means signals and radio — not movement toward the force.

  4. 4

    Board the asset

    For a landing or ground recovery, assume a non-threatening posture, secure weapons, avoid quick movement, and do not approach the recovery vehicle until instructed. Beware of rotors and propellers, especially on sloping or uneven terrain, and secure loose equipment that could be caught in them.

Hoist recovery. Use eye protection if available. Let the metal on the rescue device contact the surface before you touch it — static discharge can injure you. Sit or kneel for stability while donning the device, put the safety strap under the armpits, keep the cable in front of you, and keep hands clear of all hardware and connectors. Signal readiness with a thumbs up, a vigorous cable shake, or a radio call; drag your feet on the ground to decrease oscillation; and do not assist during the hoist or when pulled into the vehicle — follow the crew's instructions.
05Captivity

Surviving CaptivityOut of Captivity: 1,967 days in the Colombian jungle, and the mental tools that outlasted them.

The memoir of three Americans held by the FARC from 2003 to 2008 is a field study in survival psychology under the longest odds: no rescue timetable, forced marches, chains, and years of uncertainty. Their experience confirms what the manuals teach — that in prolonged ordeal the battle is almost entirely mental, and it is won through structure, goals, and the people beside you.

Routine is armor

Imposed silence and separation were calculated to break composure, so the captives kept to as strict a routine as possible — waking, meals, classes, exercise at fixed times. When everything else is controlled by the captor, a self-imposed schedule is the one thing that remains yours, and it shortens the day.

Mental escape

One captive sketched the floor plan of his family's house like a blueprint, furniture included, and several times a day studied it, visualizing what his family was doing at that exact moment. The captives "ranged far more freely in our minds than we did physically" — in conversation, memory, and imagined tasks, they were beyond the walls.

Set ascending goals

Exercise became the anchor: walking loops grew from 60 laps to 150 around the camp perimeter; a stepping routine started at 30 minutes a day, was raised to 35 when it became easy, and reached 50. Small, measurable, increasing goals convert endless time into progress and return a sense of control.

One day at a time

When the thought of six years became unbearable, a smuggled scrap of paper carried the message that steadied the group: "We are not forgotten. People are looking for us. One day at a time. We will go home." Survival in captivity is not endured in years; it is endured in days.

  • Collective strength: the three men treated their unity as the one asset they could not risk — defiance was weighed against the threat of permanent separation, and nonconfrontation became deliberate strategy, not surrender.
  • Believe in something: belief began with "mostly myself and my ability to endure this" — faith in your own endurance is the foundation every other coping skill is built on.
  • Physical condition is psychological condition: after a forty-day march left them weakened, they swore never to be that frail again; fitness guarded independence of mind as much as of body.
  • Do not stop communicating: whispered encouragement at the food pots, notes passed on scraps of paper — any contact that kept the connection going, because isolation is the captor's most corrosive weapon.
Fear of the unknown beats the known. Training reduces fear of the unknown and gives self-confidence — the manuals' promise, proven across nearly five and a half years of jungle captivity.
