12.1Home Readiness

The concentric-ring preparedness planThe urban kit manual's core idea: gear arranged in rings around your body — pockets, bag, desk, car, home — each ring bigger and more capable, every item legal to carry and in daily use rather than sealed in a box "for emergencies".

Start with your own scenarios
  • Audit the risks where you actually live: earthquake, flood, storm, fire, civil disturbance, infrastructure failure — the manual's author maps Seattle's faults, bridges, and volcano before choosing a single item. Copy the method, not his kit.
  • Check legality and practicality: the best tool is worthless if it gets you detained; a 4-inch knife legal in his state, not a machete.
  • The five needs that drive every kit: first aid, rescue of trapped people, shelter — immediately; then communication and transportation home.
  • Goal order after a disaster at work: exit the building safely, treat yourself then others, contact family, get home, then serve the community.
The "on-body" ring in one paragraph: a wool vest (insulation that works wet, with pockets that hide kit in plain sight), a boiled-wool hat with a tube tent rolled inside, bandana, Swiss Army knife, sewing kit with kevlar thread, N-95 mask, latex gloves, mini first-aid, firesteel, whistle, P-38 can opener, flashlight batteries, 10 ft of paracord — and paper: contact lists, a bike map of walking routes home, radio frequencies.
Household water & food math
  • Water: store a minimum of one gallon (about 4 L) per person per day, for drinking and minimal hygiene — two weeks minimum for shelter-in-place; more in hot climates. Date and rotate; treat with the chapter-03 methods when rotation lapses.
  • Food tiers: pantry everyday food you actually eat (rotate it); bulk staples (rice, beans, oils, salt); and a no-cook grab tier for evacuations.
  • When utilities fail: know where the water heater drain and the house water shutoff are; the tank itself is days of drinking water. Sanitation planning (bucket toilet, liners, disinfectant) fails first in every real event.
  • Lighting and power: headlamps over candles, batteries by type, a small solar panel or hand-crank for radios; keep a battery or hand-crank AM/FM radio — broadcast instructions outlast phone networks.
  • Documents go-bag: copies of ID, insurance, prescriptions, deeds; cash in small bills — card networks are the first thing to die and the last to return.
The family plan
  • Two meeting points: one near home, one outside the neighbourhood — phones fail, plans don't.
  • An out-of-area contact everyone calls: long-distance lines often work when local ones don't.
  • Utility shutoff drills — water, gas, electricity — everyone old enough to reach them learns to shut them.
  • Drill the plan twice a year; an undrilled plan is a wish.
12.2Fallout

Fallout physics, from Kearny's manualCresson Kearny's Nuclear War Survival Skills was written to dismantle panic with arithmetic. Its core numbers, kept honest.

Radiation decays fast

From a dose rate of 1,000 R/hr one hour after the explosion: about 7 hours to reach 100 R/hr, 48 hours to reach 10 R/hr, and roughly two weeks to 1 R/hr. Rapid at first, slower later — the 7:10 rule of thumb: every sevenfold increase in time cuts the dose rate tenfold.

Time in shelter

Most shelter occupants could safely stop full-time sheltering within two weeks, working outside for increasing hours — with a meter or reliable broadcast information. The mistake both ways: emerging too early, or staying down so long that water and sanitation fail.

Dose and survival

About 450 R whole-body is lethal for half of healthy adults; under bad post-attack conditions, perhaps half die at ~350 R. The body repairs most damage if daily doses stay moderate: 6 R/day for two months does not incapacitate a healthy person who starts under 100 R total.

Shielding — mass between you and fallout
  • Protection Factor (PF) is the ratio: PF 200 means the person inside receives 1/200th of the outdoor dose. A two-week outdoor dose of 20,000 R becomes 100 R inside.
  • Each halving-thickness of material halves the radiation reaching you — packed earth is the classic; five successive halving-thicknesses divide exposure 32-fold.
  • The best home spot: a basement corner against earth, or the centre of a large building's middle floor — distance plus mass. The manual's expedient shelters (door-covered trenches, log-roofed trenches) reach PF 100–200 with hand tools in a day.
  • Breathe easy: properly designed shelter air is safe — fallout particles are filtered by even simple intake arrangements; the danger is gamma from deposited particles, not the air itself.
After fallout
  • Decontaminate by removal, not by waiting: brush fallout dust off clothing before entering shelter; hair and shoes carried most of the dose in historical exposures.
  • Water and food: covered containers are safe; deep wells and covered cisterns unaffected. Peel or wash produce from exposed gardens; rain washes particles off roofs to lower positions — those lower positions are now the hot spots.
  • Potassium iodide (KI) saturates the thyroid against radioactive iodine only — a narrow, specific protection for that one organ, taken when advised, not a general radiation antidote.
Historical reference, not current doctrine: Kearny wrote for a specific Cold War threat model. The physics of decay and shielding is permanent; operational guidance should come from current civil-defence authorities in your own country.
12.3Pandemic & Shelter-In-Place

Isolation at homeDistilled from the HHS pandemic plan's household guidance: the family is both the household's patient ward and its clean zone, and the boundary between them is procedure.

The sick room protocol
  • One room, one bathroom if possible, for the sick; keep them out of common areas; the household's most vulnerable member never enters.
  • One designated carer — fewer people crossing the boundary means fewer infections; mask within 2 m of the patient, gloves for contact with body fluids, hand-washing on entering and leaving.
  • Linens and dishes washed hot and separately; hard surfaces in the sick room daily with household disinfectant; waste bagged inside the room.
  • Watch for the danger signs that convert home care to hospital care: breathing difficulty, persistent chest pain or pressure, confusion, bluish lips, inability to keep fluids down.
Supplies for two-to-three weeks
  • Fluids with electrolytes — the actual treatment for most febrile illness at home; oral rehydration salts are the highest-value item in the cabinet.
  • Fever and pain medication (acetaminophen and/or ibuprofen) dosed for every family member's age and weight; a working thermometer per floor.
  • Masks, gloves, soap, disinfectant, tissues, paper towels — the consumables everyone runs out of first.
  • Two weeks of routine prescriptions filled ahead; the plan's quiet insight — pharmacy runs are how isolation fails.
  • Food that needs no refrigeration and little cooking; the power and the cook may both be sick.
Shelter in place or evacuate? The decision test
SituationDefault answer
Structure sound, utilities on or recoverable, no immediate hazard approachingShelter in place — roads are the dangerous place in the first hours
Fire, gas leak, rising water, or structural damageLeave — immediately, on foot if traffic is stopped
Ordered evacuationGo early, with the go-bag; late evacuation is how people die in cars
Chemical/radiological release ("shelter-in-place" order)Inside, upwind floor, windows and vents sealed, radio on — driving through a plume is worse than sealing a room
