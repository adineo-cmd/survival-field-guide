04.4Fire, In Depth

Fire craft from the bushcraft textsGraves' complete method: fire with one match in any weather, fire without smoke or flame, and the full friction-fire technique with the punk test that tells you if your wood is right.

One match, in heavy rain
  1. 1

    Never gather kindling from the ground after rain or dew — it's soaked. Pull thin dead twigs, match-thickness, from standing bushes; they're dry inside.

  2. 2

    Hold a big bundle in your hand and light the far end with the match, turning the bundle until all twigs blaze, then lay it in the fireplace and feed gradually larger sticks.

  3. 3

    When even twigs are sodden: split a branch that hasn't lain on the ground, strip the wet outer layer, and shave the dry inner wood into curls along its whole length. Make 4–6 such fire-sticks; light the curled ends — they flame instantly and their heat dries the damp twigs you add on top.

  4. 4

    In wind or downpour, light the twigs or fire-sticks inside the tent or a shelter, and carry them out laid on their side in your billy.

Smokeless and flameless fire
  • Smoke is incomplete combustion. Fed continuously with 3 mm dry twigs that catch almost instantly, a fire burns nearly all flame and no smoke haze — light it under (not against) a tree and the canopy disperses the rest.
  • Flameless heat is charcoal: start a small flame fire, then feed it charcoal gathered from half-burnt stumps. Fanned, it throws great heat with no visible flame — a pierced kerosene tin makes the brazier, or a stone surround hides the glow.
  • Two fires from one match: split the match. Pin-point just below a wooden match's head splits it into two heads; paper matches peel from the butt end toward the head. Each half lights fine tinder.
Tinder — the spark catcher
  • The tinder test: push a glowing coal into the material and blow — if the fire extends through it, it qualifies.
  • Natural tinders: dry beaten grass, finely teased bark, palm fibre — improve them by beating and shredding until finely fibred; a light dusting of fine charcoal dust improves spark-catching further.
  • The bark "slow match": some barks smoulder slowly along a cord for hours — a carried coal that saves your matches.
  • Carry tinder in a waterproof bag. Prime it loose — tightly packed tinder smothers ("drowns") the flame it's meant to catch.
Spark & lens methods
  • Flint and steel needs little skill: sparks must fall on tinder, then be blown to a coal, then to flame. Cigarette-lighter flint beats natural flint — two set in a perspex handle is the emergency kit.
  • Iron pyrites struck against steel or itself throws sparks lasting a full second; quartz works but is harder.
  • Magnifying glass: focus the sun onto a tinder ball's extra-fine core; powdered charcoal at the focal point helps it catch. A concave mirror is better still.
  • Fire piston (Southeast Asia): sudden compression of air in a bone or bamboo cylinder ignites tinder in the piston head — diesel ignition, centuries early.

The bow drill, completeThe easiest friction method — Graves gives dimensions, body position, and the punk test that predicts success before you sweat.

  1. 1

    The set

    Bow (thong of leather lace or greenhide), drill or spindle, headpiece bearing-block, and footpiece hearth. Same wood for drill and hearth — willow and non-resinous pines are the named choices. Bore a shallow hole 12 mm from the hearth's edge, and cut an undercut V at least 3 mm into the hole's edge — the V collects the punk and lets the coal drop onto tinder below.

  2. 2

    Thong & tension

    Twist the drill under the thong so the drill finishes on the thong's outer side, with the handle-side thong above the drill. Wrong wrap and the thong crosses itself and cuts within strokes. Hold the bow with little and ring fingers outside the thong — squeezing them adds tension mid-stroke.

  3. 3

    Body position

    Kneel on the right knee, ball of the left foot on the footpiece. Left hand holds the headpiece with the wrist braced against the left shin — the bearing must not wobble. Lubricate the headpiece socket with pencil graphite, fat, or (Graves, honestly) earwax. Shell or smooth stone bearings burn less than wood.

  4. 4

    The punk test

    Rotate slowly with light pressure until smoke and a fine brown powder — "punk" — grind out in a dark ring. Rub it: suitable wood feels slightly gritty, then rubs to a silky face-powder smoothness. Gritty-only punk never makes a coal; test before you commit energy.

  5. 5

    Coal to flame

    Tinder bundle under the V. When smoke flows freely and the V is full of punk, add headpiece pressure and 20–30 fast strokes; lift the drill cleanly. Fold tinder over and blow gently — a continuing blue thread of smoke means coal. Fold fully over, keep blowing, and quick puffs bring flame. A pinch of charcoal or grit in the hearth hole speeds the spark.

Fire lays & the woodshed rule
  • Teepee for quick flame and boiling; log cabin for long steady cooking coals; trench for wind and concealment; long fire aligned with the body for overnight warmth, reflector behind.
  • Build the woodshed before the rain: pull down standing dead branches — never ground wood — and stock a covered pile. Only then is there dry kindling after the storm.
  • Always split-test fuel: damp outside, dry within — split and shave to the dry core for fire-sticks rather than rejecting the whole log.
Practice under dry conditions teaches you nothing. Graves' entire fire curriculum is aimed at the wet day: the confidence that matters is one-match fire in rain — a skill only built by rehearsing in it.
