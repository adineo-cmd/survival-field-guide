Navigation — know where you areGetting lost is a thinking error before it's a map error. Know north, know your handrails, and know when to stop moving.

With a compass

Set your bearing, hold the compass level, rotate your body until the red needle sits in the orienting arrow, and walk to the direction-of-travel line. Pick a landmark on that line and walk to it — compasses drift, handrails don't.

With a stick — the sun

Stand a stick in flat ground; mark the shadow tip. Wait 15 minutes, mark again. The line between marks runs west → east (first mark is west). Stand with the west mark to your left: you're facing north. Southern hemisphere, reverse it.

With the stars

North: extend the Big Dipper's two pointer stars about five times their gap to reach Polaris. South: lengthen the Southern Cross's long axis 4.5 times and drop straight to the horizon. An analog watch works by day: point the hour hand at the sun — the bisector to 12 is south.

When you realise you're lost
  1. 1

    S.T.O.P. — exactly as chapter 01. Fear walks people in circles; studies show lost travellers drift reliably to the left.

  2. 2

    If people know where you are or you told someone your plan — stay put. A stationary person is a findable person; a signal at a known location beats a track no one can follow.

  3. 3

    If you must move, follow handrails: rivers, ridgelines, power lines, fences. Rivers lead downhill, and downhill eventually leads to people, roads, or the coast.

  4. 4

    Mark your line of travel — stack cairns, break branches upward — so you can backtrack and searchers can read your direction.

Folk signs — use as hints, never as proof
  • Moss grows on the north side — sometimes. Shade, moisture, and wind confuse it.
  • Snow melts on the south face — only in consistent northern-hemisphere sun.
  • Rings on tree stumps are denser on the sunnier side — again, only loosely.

These are suggestions, not navigation. One solid celestial method beats all of them combined.

The best navigation tool is a habit: every trip, tell someone your route and return time, and look backward every few hundred metres so the way home stays familiar.
