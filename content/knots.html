09.1Rope Craft

The rope comes firstBefore the knots: what rope is, how it loses strength, and how the books say to care for it. A knot is never as strong as the rope it's tied in — a simple overhand knot weakens line by about 50 percent.

Natural fibre

Manila, sisal, hemp and cotton hold knots well because their fibres grip — but they rot. Store natural rope dry, off the ground; if it smells musty or shows dark patches, downgrade it to non-critical jobs only. Whip the ends: melted finishing does not exist for plant fibre.

Synthetic rope

Nylon stretches and absorbs shock (anchor lines, towing); polypropylene floats but degrades in sunlight; kernmantle climbing rope carries people. Synths are slippery: many classical knots spill in them, so the books consistently add a stopper or extra turn where a natural-fibre knot would hold alone.

Strength & retirement

Every knot reduces breaking strength; bends and loops typically retain 70–95 percent, the overhand family the least. Shock loading is the silent killer — a load applied fast can part rope rated several times the static weight. Retire any rope that has held a hard shock load, shows glazing (melt-sheen), flat spots, or a cut strand.

Fisherman's discipline applies to all cordage: wet the knot with saliva before tightening (heat-friction damages line), pull every end tight, trim tags close — and never burn a tag end on fishing line; the heat weakens the knot.
09.2Bindings & Bends

Binding knots & rope-joinersBindings hold things together; bends join two ropes. Learn five of these and you can do almost everything a camp asks of you.

Reef (square) knot

Right over left, left over right — both twists must have the same end finishing on top or you've tied the weaker granny knot; the finished knot is symmetrical, which is your visual check. Binds bandages, parcels, sack mouths. Never a bend: it spills easily when the ends aren't supported, and fails outright in slippery synthetic rope or uneven thicknesses.

Surgeon's knot

The reef knot with an extra pass on the first twist. Surgeons use it for sutures because it holds slippery thread; campers use it for the same reason on artificial-fibre twine and for completing lashings in synthetics. Still a binding — never join two ropes with it.

Constrictor knot

A clove hitch with one extra tuck, and it lives up to the name: the strongest simple binding there is, for bag mouths, quick whippings and holding splice strands. Effectively permanent — plan to cut it off. Never on a limb or bandage: it tightens under load and will not let go.

Sheet bend (+ double)

The way to join two ropes — including ropes of different sizes, and to tie a rope to a tarpaulin loop or net bight. Form a bight in the stiffer rope, pass the other through and around, both ends exiting the same side. In slippery rope or a big size mismatch, take the thin end round twice (double sheet bend). Tie off the tags with overhands if it will flap.

Double fisherman's (grapevine)

Two double overhands, each tied around the other rope. The join the climbing manuals trust — it never spills once set, works wet, dry, smooth or laid, and still unties after big loads. Leave generous tags: the ends pull through during tightening. Bulky, and uses a lot of cord.

Tape / water knot

Two interlaced overhands for joining webbing tape into slings — keep the tape flat and untwisted so the surfaces friction against each other. In rope it's the water knot: secure but near-impossible to untie after loading. The standard for homemade climbing slings.

Whipping a rope end
  • Use twine significantly thinner than the rope, strong and high-friction — waxed or tarred natural fibre is best.
  • Re-lay any frayed strands, then lay a loop of twine along the rope and wind tight, close turns over it, working toward the rope's end.
  • Pass the tag through the loop and draw both ends down under the turns; trim. The whipping should be at least as long as the rope is thick, finishing a little before the end.
  • Synthetic rope can instead be fused with a flame or hot blade — but whip large-diameter synthetics anyway; fused lumps snag in pulleys and tarp eyelets.
09.3Loops & Hitches

Loops & hitchesLoops make attachment points that don't slip closed; hitches tie a rope to something else. Between them they rig every shelter, guyline and rescue system you will ever build.

Bowline

The fixed loop of the sea: rabbit comes up out of his hole, around the tree, back down his hole. Strong, unties even after heavy loading, and the end must finish inside the loop (outside is the weaker cowboy bowline). Back it with an overhand on the tag in synthetic rope. Loosens when unloaded and flapping — check it.

Running bowline

A bowline tied around its own standing part: the loop slides closed and makes a snare, a grab-line for drifting gear, or a throw-line over a high branch. The loop that tightens but never jams — release by pushing the bowline's collar back.

Double figure eight

Tie a figure eight well back from the end, then retrace the knot with the tag — the loop climbers tie into harnesses with. Its symmetry is its check: if it doesn't look like an 8, it's wrong. Stronger than the bowline in synthetic rope; not for three-way pulls.

Alpine butterfly

The loop for the middle of a rope when the ends and the loop may each be pulled in different directions — the three-way pull that ruins a figure eight. The isolation loop for hauling, hand-lines, and tying into a rope between two people.

Clove hitch

The start-and-finish knot of every lashing, tied at right angles to a spar — two passes around, tuck under the first. Fast, adjustable, can be tied one-handed or dropped over a post as two interleaved loops. Fails on slippery spars, thin poles (under 2× rope diameter), square posts, or where the load direction wanders.

Round turn & two half hitches

The anchor attachment when the object is smooth, irregular or moving: a full round turn takes the load off the hitches and adds friction, then two half hitches (same direction, forming a clove hitch on the standing part) lock it. Holds under tension while you tie it — the property that makes it the ridge-line knot.

Rolling hitch

Grips a spar or another rope for a pull at an oblique angle, and the books' choice for adjustable tent guylines (the taut-line hitch is its scouting variant): slide it, then load it and it holds. Tie the second turn pulled up over the shoulder of the first; it must be tied under tension to seat.

Trucker's hitch

A movable loop in the line becomes a pulley, roughly doubling the tension you can put on a ridge-line or load lash: fix one end, pull through the loop, haul, then lock with half hitches. Skip it in natural fibre — the rope-on-rope sawing damages the line when tightened hard.

Prusik knot

A loop of thin cord wrapped around a thicker rope: grips under load, slides when unweighted. Two or three prusik loops let you climb a rope or a spar — the crevasse self-rescue knot. Cord should be smooth, supple and no more than half the diameter of the rope it grips. Load gently: a shock load makes it slide and can melt its own surface.

Timber hitch

For dragging: wrap the end around the log and twist the tag back on itself — on the rope's own standing part, not around the log — several times. The twists give grip; the longer the drag and the smoother the bark, the more twists. Starts diagonal lashings where poles must be sprung together.

09.4Lashings

Lashings — rope becomes structureSpars plus lashings equal furniture, shelters, bridges and tripods. Every lashing is the same three-act shape: fix, wrap, frap.

  1. 1

    Fix — clove hitch & twist

    Open with a clove hitch on one spar, then twist the tag around the standing part so the start can't work loose as you wrap.

  2. 2

    Wrap — the body of the lashing

    Neat, tight, side-by-side turns. Square lashing wraps across the joint of spars that already touch; diagonal lashing pulls together spars that spring apart — timber hitch first, then wraps across both.

  3. 3

    Frap — tighten between the spars

    Two or three turns between the spars, pulling the wraps hard onto each other — this is what gives the lashing its rigidity. Close with a clove hitch on the far spar.

The four camp lashings
  • Square lashing — two spars at right angles that touch: table legs, camp furniture, framework. Three or four wraps, two fraps.
  • Diagonal lashing — spars that cross at an angle or must be pulled together: the X-braces of a tower or shelter frame. Timber hitch start, wraps in both directions.
  • Shear lashing — spars laid parallel, then opened into an A: shear legs for a washing stand, or the two legs of an A-frame. Wraps loosely, fraps between, spread after.
  • Figure-of-eight (tripod) lashing — three spars, rope woven over-under in a figure eight seven or eight times, frap between each pair, and — unlike other lashings — keep the wraps loose: the lashing tightens itself when the legs are opened into the tripod.
Japanese Mk II shortcut: double the rope, bight behind the upright, then cross the two ends around the spars in alternating wraps — fastest lashing to tie, the books' pick for bamboo and thin spars, finished by reef-knotting the two ends.
09.5Fishing Knots

Fishing knotsLine is the most hostile cordage there is — thin, slippery and weak — so fishing knots are lessons in what actually holds. Ratings are retained line strength.

Improved clinch — ~95%

Line to hook or lure: five turns of the tag around the standing end, back through the loop formed at the eye, then through the big loop — that final tuck is the "improvement". Wet, tighten by pulling standing end and tag, trim close. Best under 20 lb line.

Palomar — over 95%

Double the line first, pass the loop through the eye, tie a simple overhand around the standing double, then drop the loop over the hook and pull everything tight. Uses more line and tangles more easily, but it's the strongest common attachment and works past 20 lb test.

Non-slip loop

Like an improved clinch but finished through the descending loop, leaving the hook free to swing in a fixed loop — for lures that need action. The choice in heavier line where a clinched knot would stiffen the presentation.

Blood knot

Joining two similar lines: twist the tags around each other's standing part five to seven turns, pass back through the middle. The tapering twists make it smooth through rod guides; the constituent of the double fisherman's in rope.

Double surgeon's loop

The quick join for lines of different sizes: lay the lines together, tie a double overhand with both, pass through a second time, wet and draw tight. Not the neatest, but fast, reliable and easy in cold hands — which is exactly when fishing happens.

Uni knot

The one-knot system: with more or fewer turns it serves as hook attachment, line-to-line join or loop. Four turns for heavy line, six-plus for light; wet before seating. If you learn only one fishing knot, the books say, learn this one.
