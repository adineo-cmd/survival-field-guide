CHAPTER 06 — PLANTS

Edible & Medicinal Plants

The plant library: identification-first profiles of the major edible wild plants of North America with their toxic lookalikes, mushroom safety, and the herbal preparation methods the medicinal guides actually describe.

06POSITIVE ID
OR PASS
01Foundations

The Wild Table, Earned CarefullyIdentification before appetite, always.

Nearly every North American landscape — lawn, fence row, marsh, forest edge — carries a standing pantry of edible wild plants. The old field guides agree on the central lesson: wild food rewards certainty and punishes guesses. A forager who can name twenty common plants positively will never go hungry in temperate country; a forager who samples on faith may not survive one mistake. Learn a few plants thoroughly, in all their stages, and leave the rest alone.

  • Know it whole: study each plant through its full season — leaf, flower, fruit, and root — before you ever eat it.
  • Harvest clean: gather away from roadsides, sprayed lawns, and industrial ground; plants concentrate what is in the soil.
  • Eat in moderation: even tested familiar foods such as green apples and wild onions cause nausea, cramps, or diarrhea in quantity on an empty stomach.
  • Respect the abundance: do not strip a stand; take a fraction and let the colony seed and spread.
The Universal Edibility Test

The Army survival manual's procedure for an unknown plant — a last resort when no recognized food is available. Each plant part requires more than 24 hours to test, so only test plants abundant enough to be worth the effort.

  1. 1

    Separate and fast

    Test only one part of one plant at a time — leaves, stems, roots, buds, and flowers are tested separately. Eat nothing for 8 hours beforehand.

  2. 2

    Skin check first

    Smell for strong or acid odors, then hold a piece against the inside of your elbow or wrist for 15 minutes to test for contact poisoning.

  3. 3

    Lip, then tongue

    Prepare the part as you intend to eat it. Touch a pinch to your lip for 3 minutes; if no burning or itching, hold it on your tongue for 15 minutes.

  4. 4

    Chew, do not swallow

    Chew a pinch thoroughly and hold it in your mouth 15 minutes. No burning, itching, numbing, or stinging? Swallow it.

  5. 5

    Wait eight hours

    Take nothing by mouth except purified water. If ill effects appear, induce vomiting and drink plenty of water.

  6. 6

    Confirm with a meal

    Eat a quarter cup prepared the same way and wait another 8 hours. No reaction, and that part, prepared that way, is safe.

Test every part separately — a plant may have edible leaves and toxic roots. A part proven safe cooked may be harmful raw. Individual reactions vary between people.
  • Reject on sight: milky or discolored sap; beans, bulbs, or seeds in pods; a bitter or soapy taste; spines, fine hairs, or thorns.
  • Reject on sight: foliage resembling dill, carrot, parsnip, or parsley — the poisonous hemlocks and water hemlock live there.
  • Reject on sight: almond scent in woody parts and leaves; grain heads with pink, purplish, or black spurs; a three-leafed growth pattern.
02Yard and Field

Weeds of Disturbed GroundThe most reliable greens grow closest to the door.

The common weeds of gardens, paths, and old fields are the beginner's pantry: abundant, unmistakable, and nourishing as cooked greens. Every one followed human settlement across the continent.

Dandelion

Basal rosette of deeply toothed leaves — "lion's teeth" — milky stalks, and a solitary yellow flower head on a hollow stem (Taraxacum officinale). Leaves are best in early spring before the flowers open, eaten raw or boiled like spinach with the water changed once. Young roots are palatable, and the roasted root makes a credible coffee substitute. Season: greens April to June, roots autumn.

Common Plantain

A stemless rosette of broad, parallel-ribbed leaves with green flower spikes from the center (Plantago major); narrow-leaved Plantago lanceolata is equally useful. Formerly eaten as a potherb in Europe and Asia, still gathered as a spring green. The youngest leaves are tender; older ones grow stringy but boil down serviceably. Season: spring and early summer. Crushed leaves are the traditional dressing for insect stings.

Chickweed

A low, straggling annual with a single line of hair down the stem, small paired leaves, and tiny white star flowers (Stellaria media). It thrives in cool weather — spring and again in autumn — forming dense mats on garden edges. Properly boiled it makes an excellent green much resembling spinach in flavor, and the tender tips can go raw into salads. Gather only from clean ground, since mats hug the soil.

Purslane

A fleshy, trailing annual with reddish stems, thick spatulate leaves, and small yellow flowers that open only on sunny mornings (Portulaca oleracea). Found in nearly every old garden. Cooked and served like spinach it keeps a pleasant slight acid tang; the crisp stems also go into salads and soups, and pickle well. A first-rate summer green when others have bolted.

Lamb's Quarters

A coarse, pale blue-green annual, two to seven feet tall, with mealy-white undersides to the diamond-shaped leaves — the "goosefoot" (Chenopodium album). Probably the most common weed usable as human food; it famously follows potato cultivation. Plants six to ten inches high are succulent and tender, excellent boiled as wild spinach; the seeds are edible as a grain. Season: late spring through summer.

Sow Thistle and Burdock

Two more European settlers worth knowing. Sow thistle (Sonchus oleraceus) is a dandelion relative with clasping leaves and yellow flowers; the young leaves are a wholesome potherb after one boiling. Burdock (Arctium lappa, A. minus) is a big rough weed of waste land whose first-year root, peeled and boiled, is a classic old-world vegetable. Season: spring greens, autumn roots.

  • Do not confuse young thistles and sow thistle leaves with those of wild lettuces that drip white sap — test unfamiliar greens.
Boiling in one or two changes of water tames the bitterness of nearly all wild potherbs and is the safest default preparation for greens.
03Marsh and Shore

Wetland StaplesCattail country is grocery country.

Lake, stream, and brackish-water margins held some of the most important Native food plants on the continent, and they produce in every season — shoots in spring, pollen and green spikes in early summer, starch-rich roots through winter.

Cattail

Grasslike strap leaves to six feet, with the familiar brown seed spike atop a stalk (Typha latifolia, T. angustifolia). A four-season plant: peeled young shoots raw or cooked as "Cossack asparagus"; the green female spike boiled and eaten like corn on the cob; abundant yellow pollen shaken out as flour; rhizomes pounded in water to settle out starch for baking. Season: shoots April to May, spikes and pollen June, roots whenever the ground is workable.

Great Bulrush

Tall, smooth, olive-green round stems from creeping rhizomes in shallow water (Scirpus validus), at swamp and pond margins across nearly all of North America. The starchy rootstocks were a staple for the Indians of California, Arizona, and the upper Missouri valley — dug, dried, and ground into meal. The tender white bases of young stems, pulled in spring, are eaten raw or cooked. Season: shoot bases spring, roots autumn to winter.

Arrowhead, or Wapatoo

Familiar in shallow ponds and sluggish streams, with long-petioled arrow-shaped leaves and white three-petaled flowers, July to September (Sagittaria latifolia). The tuberous roots were so prized that western tribes ranked them, after camas, the most valuable native food; Lewis and Clark recorded tribes subsisting chiefly on them. Tubers are boiled or roasted in the ashes like potatoes. Season: best from late summer through winter.

  • Tuckahoe or green arrow arum (Peltandra virginica) grows in the same mud with arrowhead leaves; its raw root is violently acrid and must be thoroughly dried and cooked. Never eat arum tubers raw.

04Trees and Shrubs

Bark, Sap, and MastWinter food grows on trees.

When herbs are buried under snow, trees keep giving: bark and sap in late winter, catkins in spring, nuts from late summer on. Identification is safest in any season — but be sure of the species before you cut, and strip twigs rather than girdling a trunk you need alive.

Pine

Needles in bundles of one to five, resinous odor, sticky sap (Pinus species) — a combination no other needled tree shares. The seeds of all species are edible, and green needle tea is a frontline source of vitamin C. Young male cones collected in spring are boiled or baked as a survival food; peel thin twigs and chew the juicy, sugary inner bark. Season: any time of year.

Oak and Acorns

Alternate leaves and acorn fruit; white oaks (no bristle-tipped leaves, rough upper bark, acorns ripening in one year) taste better than bitter red oaks (Quercus species). Shell the acorns and soak them one to two days to leach the bitterness — wood ashes in the water speed the process — then boil, or grind to flour for baking; baked until very dark they make a coffee substitute. Season: autumn drop.

  • Tannic acid makes unleached acorns bitter, and excessive amounts can lead to kidney failure. Leach every batch before eating.

Birch

Black or cherry birch (Betula lenta) carries the sweet wintergreen aroma in bark and twigs; yellow birch (B. lutea) is milder, and paper birch (B. papyrifera) yields a leaf tea recorded in the Maine woods. Spring cambium of young trees is a sweet morsel, and April sap — half as sweet as maple's — can be boiled down for sugar. Wintergreen tea from the twigs reportedly saved starving soldiers on a Civil War retreat. Season: twigs any time, sap in early spring.

Willow

Water-loving shrubs and trees with narrow pointed leaves and pliant shoots (Salix species). The succulent young shoots of early spring are peeled and eaten raw, and young leaves are among the richest known sources of vitamin C — several times an orange. Inner bark is survival food everywhere willows grow, and its salicin is the traditional fever tea at the root of aspirin. Season: spring shoots and leaves.

  • Strip shoots from willow only; strip similar waterside shrubs carelessly and you may take a branch of poison sumac or elder. Check leaf and bud before you cut.

Sassafras

A small tree famous for three leaf shapes on one plant — unlobed, mitten-shaped, and three-lobed — with aromatic bark and roots (Sassafras variifolium). Root bark and twigs made the original "root" beer and a pleasant spring tea; the dark blue fruits on red stalks were nibbled by country children. Season: spring roots and shoots, late-summer fruit.

Walnut and Hickory

Stately forest nuts worth the labor: black walnut (Juglans nigra) with rough green-hulled fruit and pinnate leaves, and the hickories (Carya species) with sweet kernels in thick shells. The green walnut hull holds a resinous irritant like poison ivy that blisters lips and tongue — heat destroys it on roasting, and gloves make husking safe. Season: autumn drop.

Take bark food from twigs and branches rather than the trunk: cutting a ring around a trunk kills the tree that feeds you.
05Fruits and Bulbs

Berries, and the Smell-Test BulbSweet things with rules attached.

Bramble and heath fruits are the gateway wild foods — few plants are as easy to name with certainty. Two rules govern this section: elderberry is cooked-only, and no bulb goes in the mouth until it answers the onion-smell test.

Blackberry and Dewberry

Prickly arching canes with compound leaves and aggregate fruit that pulls free of the receptacle, ripening red to shining black (Rubus species). Open sunny margins of woods, streams, and roads throughout temperate regions. Fruit eaten raw or cooked; peeled spring shoots are a trail nibble, and dried root-bark tea is the old remedy for diarrhea. Season: mid to late summer.

Raspberry

The same Rubus habit with a rounder, hollow-centered fruit that slips off the core when picked, red or black with a pale bloom; there is even an arctic species. Berries eaten raw, dried, or cooked; the leaves make a pleasant, widely used tea. Seek canes at the same sunlit edges as blackberry. Season: summer, earliest in the south.

Blueberry and Huckleberry

Low to tall shrubs, ten inches to head height, with alternate simple leaves and many-seeded berries of dark blue, black, or red, often powder-bloomed (Vaccinium, Gaylussacia species). They prefer open sun — burns, ridgelines, barrens — and berry in mid to late summer. Fruits edible raw and drying well for winter; huckleberry seeds are noticeable, blueberry seeds not. No dangerous lookalike shares this fruit shape.

Elderberry — cooked only

A many-stemmed shrub to twelve feet with opposite pinnate leaves, broad flat-topped clusters of fragrant white June flowers, then deep blue-black berries (Sambucus canadensis). Flowers are soaked for a cooling drink; the berries make pies, jellies, and jams, best blended with tarter fruit — always cooked.

  • Stems, leaves, roots, and raw berries are poisonous — every part except the flowers and thoroughly ripe, cooked fruit. Red-berried elder (S. racemosa) is bitter and unpleasant even when cooked.

Wild Onion and Garlic

Slender channeled leaves from a small bulb and a nodding umbel of rose or white blossoms (Allium cernuum, A. canadense, and kin) on banks and hillsides across nearly the whole country. The strong penetrating odor is the identification. Bulbs and young leaves are eaten raw or cooked — strong bulbs are best parboiled — and they pickle excellently.

  • Death camas (Zigadenus species) grows in the same open habitats with grasslike leaves and six-parted flowers from a bulb — and no onion smell. All parts are very poisonous. If a bulb does not smell unmistakably of onion, discard it. Never eat an odorless "wild onion."

Wild Strawberry

A low silky-hairy plant of sunny edges with three-part leaves, white five-petaled flowers, and small intensely sweet red berries (Fragaria virginiana, F. vesca). The cultivated strawberry descends from the Virginia species. Fruit eaten fresh or dried; leaves make a mild tea. Tiny beside the garden berry and far better flavored. Season: late spring to early summer.

Every berry rule has an exception that can hurt you: teach children the specific named berries they may pick — never "all red berries are safe" or "all berries birds eat are safe." Poisonous bittersweet nightshade berries are bright, bird-taken, and toxic.
06Fungi

Mushrooms: Small List, Strict RulesBeginners should hunt kinds, not chances.

Mushroom poisoning kills differently than plant poisoning — the deadliest species taste pleasant and their symptoms arrive days late, after the damage is done. The field guide's standing advice is to hunt specific, unmistakable kinds, never to sample broadly: "There is no risk in eating wild mushrooms of proved quality... there is danger in eating all wild mushrooms indiscriminately."

Morels

The sponge mushroom: a tan to brown cap pitted with irregular honeycomb ridges, cap and stem both hollow and brittle, two to six inches tall (Morchella species). Nobody who has seen a picture confuses a true morel with anything else. Look in woods every spring — February in the deep south to late May in the north, often on old burns. Unexcelled when cooked, and they dry easily for keeping. Always cook morels; never eat them raw.

Puffballs

White, roughly spherical (or pear-shaped on wood), from golf ball to a foot across, in meadows, parks, and open woods (Calvatia and Lycoperdon species). The safety check is absolute: slice every one top to bottom — the interior must be uniformly firm and pure white. If a definite stem runs through the section, or any outline of gills or a cap appears, it is not a puffball and may be a deadly button. Use only while the flesh is white.

Shaggy Mane

A tall, cylindrical, shaggy-scaled white cap like an unrolled cigar, four to six inches high, growing in tight clusters on lawns, roadsides, and grassy places from spring to fall (Coprinus comatus). The gills darken and the cap deliquesces — literally melts into black ink — within hours of maturity, so cook only young firm specimens the day they are picked. One of the best and most easily recognized of the common edibles.

Chanterelles

A bright chrome-yellow funnel with the margin curved down and thick, prominently forked ridge-like gills running well down the stem (Cantharellus cibarius) — a shape and color "almost impossible to confuse with any other." Widely distributed in conifer and hardwood forests, rated well above average in texture and flavor. The orange-red C. aurantiacus is also edible though thinner fleshed. Season: summer into fall.

Sulphur Shelf

A bracket fungus in overlapping brilliant orange-and-sulphur shelves on standing trees, trunks, and stumps, with a uniform yellow-orange spore surface of pores rather than gills (Polyporus sulphureus). One of the "foolproof four" — found on living oaks and other hardwoods summer and fall. Slice and fry in butter; the flavor is famous for resembling chicken breast. Eat only young, moist, tender shelves growing from wood.

The Deadly Amanitas — Learn These Traits Cold

The genus Amanita — including the Death Cap (A. phalloides), Destroying Angel (A. verna), and Fly Agaric (A. muscaria) — accounts for the large majority of fatal mushroom poisonings. Its four hallmarks:

  • White spores — confirmed by spore print, not by gill color.
  • Gills free from the stem — they approach but do not touch it.
  • A ring around the upper stem, which withers and vanishes with age — examine old stems closely.
  • A cup, or volva, around the stem base — often buried and visible only if you dig out the entire stem, sometimes reduced to inconspicuous rings on a swollen base.
The Death Cap is fairly common across the North Temperate Zone, tastes pleasant, and its volva usually hides underground — the two famous warning signs are the ones most often missed. If you did not dig out the whole base, you did not check for the cup. Amanita symptoms are delayed a day or more; by the time they appear the toxins have already attacked the liver. There is no field cure.

Making a Spore Print

  1. 1

    Cut and set

    Cut the stem off just beneath the cap and stand the cap, gills down, on paper — half black, half white is best for reading pale colors.

  2. 2

    Cover and wait

    Invert a glass or bowl over it to keep the air still and moist; add a scrap of wet cotton or paper so the cap does not dry out.

  3. 3

    Read the color

    In an hour or two fallen spores trace the gill pattern. Spore color often differs from gill color — gills alone are never a reliable guide.

Rules That Do Not Bend

  • No tasting: never sample an unidentified mushroom, even a crumb.
  • Every specimen: check each individual — edible and deadly species grow intermingled; a spore print of each is the only certainty.
  • Reject the old: pass over limp, flabby, or maggoty caps; bacteria may already have rendered them harmful.
  • Cook everything: even choice edibles are unwise raw.
  • Keep a whole specimen: if someone falls ill, a complete specimen speeds treatment.
Sources

Distilled from five classic references in this collection: Edible Wild Plants (the 366-page field guide whose plant-by-plant entries supply most profiles above); Edible and Medicinal Plants and its sibling Survival Edible Medicinal Plants (the Army-style guide, source of the Universal Edibility Test, species descriptions, and caution notes); Useful Wild Plants of the United States and Canada (historic and Native American uses); and Common Edible Mushrooms (the "foolproof four," Amanita traits, and the spore-print method). 06.4Herbal Craft

Herbal preparations, with ratiosEvery dosage form in the herbal preparation guide, reduced to its working recipe: which plant parts, which solvent, how long, and how strong. Label everything — date, strength (e.g. 1:2), herb list, dosing.

Read before using anything in this chapter. Plant medicine is real medicine — it has doses, interactions and contraindications, and a misidentified plant is poison. Nothing here is medical advice or a substitute for professional care; it is a record of what the reference books teach, for context where no care is reachable. Positive plant identification is mandatory, in season, from multiple field marks.
Infusion — the "tea" (leaves & flowers)
  • Hot: 1 tsp dried herb (or 2 tbsp fresh) per cup of boiling water, covered, steeped 10 minutes. Covering holds the volatile oils in.
  • Cold: same ratios, room-temperature water, 4–8 hours — for the most fragile constituents.
  • Sun tea: room-temperature water, tightly sealed jar, sunny sill 4–8 hours.
  • Extracts vitamins, volatile oils, tannins, glycosides and sugars — the soft chemistry of soft plant parts.
Decoction — roots, bark, seeds
  • Same herb-to-water ratio as infusion, but simmered covered ~20 minutes (longer for some barks), then cooled and strained — or steeped overnight in the hot liquid and drunk in the morning.
  • Woody, resinous material needs heat and time to give up its constituents.
  • Never aluminium cookware for any herbal preparation — glass, stainless steel, or enamelled pot only.
Tincture — alcohol extraction
  • Fill a jar half to three-quarters with dried or fresh herb, top up with 80–100 proof vodka (40–50% alcohol); resinous herbs need 60–90% grain alcohol cut with distilled water as needed.
  • Tight lid, dark place, minimum 2 weeks, shaking daily — up to 6 weeks for stubborn herbs. Strain through cloth until sediment-free; bottle dark and cool. Keeps for years.
  • Dosing runs 15–30 drops three times daily to a teaspoon hourly in acute use — herb- and person-dependent. Under the tongue absorbs fastest; drop into just-boiled water and cool for children (the alcohol largely dissipates).
  • Vinegar and glycerin tinctures follow the same method (glycerite: equal parts vegetable glycerin and water) — weaker, gentler, shorter shelf life.
Poultice, fomentation, oil, salve
  • Poultice: the bruised, chewed or mashed plant itself on the injury, bandaged on — yarrow leaf on a cut, chewed plantain on a sting. Ground flax seed mixed with the herb holds it in place; warmed, it is a warm poultice.
  • Fomentation: a cloth soaked in double- or quadruple-strength infusion/decoction, wrung onto the area, covered to hold heat — never an electric pad over a wet cloth.
  • Oil: wilt fresh herbs a few hours (no surface moisture), cover with extra-virgin olive oil plus 2–3 inches, sunny window two weeks or oven/double-boiler at never above 120 °F (~50 °C) for 1–3 hours.
  • Salve: ¼ cup grated beeswax per cup of herbal oil, melted together; test consistency on a chilled spoon; optional 8 drops benzoin tincture per cup as preservative. Pour at first cloudiness; keeps months, discard at any mould.
Syrup — the child's dosage form
  • Simmer 2 oz dried herb mix in 1 quart water down to 1 pint; strain; dissolve in 1 cup raw honey per pint with gentle heat only — never cook the honey.
  • Optional 3–4 tbsp fruit brandy per cup (relaxes throat muscles) or a drop of peppermint/spearmint oil for flavour. Dark bottles, refrigerated.
06.5Plant Monographs

The medicine shelf, plant by plantHerbs with the longest documented use and the clearest cautions in the source texts — the WHO monographs where they exist, folk records where they don't.

Willow bark

Salix spp. The folk source of aspirin: bark rich in salicin (~1%) and related salicylates. Decoction of inner bark for pain, fever and inflammation — slower, gentler, harder on the stomach than the pure drug. Never with aspirin/NSAIDs, blood thinners, or in children with fever (Reye's syndrome risk, same as aspirin).

Aloe vera gel

The WHO monograph's most unambiguous topical: fresh inner gel on burns, wounds and radiation injury — cooling, wound-healing, anti-inflammatory. Split the leaf and apply directly; the yellow latex just under the skin is a strong laxative, not part of the treatment.

Yarrow

Achillea millefolium — the soldier's woundwort, named for Achilles. Crushed leaf as a styptic poultice on cuts; infusion as a bitter digestive and febrile tea. A traditional emmenagogue: avoid in pregnancy. Positive ID: feathery leaves, flat-topped flower clusters; avoid confusing with the toxic fool's parsley family lookalikes.

Plantain (the weed)

Plantago — no relation to the banana. The classic drawing poultice: chewed leaf on bee stings, nettle rash, splinters and boils draws venom and irritants out. Safe enough that the books teach it to children; grows in every trampled path in the temperate world.

Ginger root

Rhizome with a WHO monograph for nausea — including motion sickness. Fresh-chewed, as infusion, or syrup. Warming diaphoretic in colds. Heartburn at high dose; caution with anticoagulants.

Peppermint

Infusion is the settled digestive — spasms, gas, mild indigestion. The volatile oil (a drop or two in hot water, inhaled as steam) opens sinuses. Avoid strong peppermint with reflux/hiatal hernia — it relaxes the lower oesophageal sphincter.

Chamomile

Flower infusion — the gentle sedative and child-safe digestive in every folk pharmacopoeia. Compresses for skin irritation. Ragweed-family allergy is the main contraindication.

Echinacea

Root tincture at the onset of colds — the monographs support immune stimulation for early respiratory infection, not prevention and not once ill. Tingling on the tongue is normal for fresh tincture; avoid in progressive autoimmune disease.

Garlic

Raw crushed clove — antibiotic-in-a-foil tradition: antimicrobial topically (diluted) and internally, the folk wound and chest remedy. Plates blood like a mild anticoagulant — stop high doses before surgery; irritates raw tissue.

Calendula

Pot marigold petals — the infused oil and salve are the wound-care tradition for grazes and slow-healing skin; gentle enough for children. Distinct from the ornamental marigold Tagetes; use Calendula officinalis only.

Pine needle infusion

The backcountry vitamin-C tea — steep fresh green needles hot; also mildly expectorant. Skip yew (flat needles, red berries — a few needles can kill) and ponderosa pine in pregnancy.

Eucalyptus & spruce steams

A few drops of eucalyptus oil — or a handful of spruce needles — in a bowl of hot water, towel over the head: the decongestant steam of every tradition. Eucalyptus oil is poisonous swallowed, and toxic to small children even topically — steam only.

06.6Limits

When herbs are not enoughThe wilderness-medicine manual's hierarchy: herbal treatment is the last tier of care, not the first.

The tiered response
  1. 1

    Prevent: cleanliness, wound care at the moment of injury, hydration, warmth — 90% of historical herbalism was applied because prevention failed.

  2. 2

    Assess severity: bleeding that will not stop, broken bones, bites, fever with rigors, altered consciousness, spreading redness from a wound — these need evacuation, not tea.

  3. 3

    Modern pharmaceuticals first when carried: analgesics, antiseptics, oral rehydration salts and antibiotics in a proper medical kit outperform every field herbal preparation.

  4. 4

    Herbal treatment fills the gap when none of the above exist — comfort care, mild complaints, and the long game of nutrition and hygiene.

The three herbal errors that hurt people
  • Misidentification — the lookalike problem kills; hemlock mistaken for yarrow, foxglove for comfrey.
  • Dosing by folklore — "natural" has no dose ceiling; salicin, ephedra and digitalis are all natural.
  • Interactions — willow with anticoagulants, garlic before surgery, strong emmenagogues in pregnancy.
The honest summary of the sources: the WHO monographs exist precisely to separate plants with evidence from plants with stories. Where this page says "the monograph supports it", that is the strongest claim field herbalism can make.
