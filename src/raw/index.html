01Priorities

The Rule of ThreesWhen everything feels urgent, this tells you what actually kills first. Work down the list — never up it.

3
Minutes

Without air

Severe bleeding and icy water belong here too. They can kill faster than anything else — handle them immediately.

3
Hours

Without shelter

In harsh conditions — cold, wet, wind, or extreme heat — exposure becomes lethal before thirst or hunger do.

3
Days

Without water

Your body is ~60% water. Losing just 2% of it measurably degrades judgment, mood, and physical output.

3
Weeks

Without food

Food is the last priority. Hunger is uncomfortable, not urgent — don't burn energy chasing it early.

First skill: stop, don't sprintMost survival situations are made fatal by panic within the first hour. Before any technique, run this loop.

S

Stop

Sit down. Breathe slowly. Panic makes people abandon gear, walk in circles, and die within a day of safety.

T

Think

What do you actually have? Who knows where you are? How long until they expect you back?

O

Observe

Scan the terrain, weather, daylight remaining, water sources, and natural shelter before moving a step.

P

Plan

Decide one course of action and commit. Conserve energy — sweat is heat loss in cold and water loss in heat.

The survivor's mindset beats the survivor's gear. People with no equipment and a calm plan are rescued every year; people with full packs and panic are not. Your brain is the one tool you always have with you.
02Shelter

Shelter — beat the elementsHypothermia is the number-one outdoor killer, and it starts at +10 °C on a wet, windy day. Shelter is your first build, every time.

Debris hut

The all-rounder with no tools. A ridgepole frame draped with sticks, piled under 60–90 cm of dry leaves. Pile leaves inside too — you want to be the filling, not the tent.

Lean-to

Fastest windbreak and rain roof, built against a ridgepole between two trees. Reflect a fire's heat into the open side — it shelters from weather, not from cold alone.

A-frame

Two poles in an A with ribs along one side. Sheds rain well and blocks wind from one direction. Same rule as all natural shelters: thick debris bedding, small inside volume.

Snow cave

Counter-intuitive but true: snow insulates. Dig into a drift or pile and hollow it, keep the roof rounded, and always poke a fist-sized ventilation hole through — not up-slope.

Heat escapes downward too. The ground pulls heat out of you faster than the air. A 15 cm mattress of leaves, pine boughs, or grass is not optional — it's half the shelter.
Site selection
  • Build small. A shelter just bigger than your body keeps its air warm. Big shelters are cold shelters.
  • Near materials, near (but not on) water. Carry distance costs calories; building 30 m from a stream avoids cold air pooling and insects.
  • Avoid low ground — cold air and water both flow downhill and pool there overnight.
  • Avoid ridgelines and open flats — maximum wind exposure.
  • Look up before lying down. Dead standing trees and leaning branches — "widowmakers" — fall without warning.
  • Check for water courses: a dry wash can become a river in rain you can't yet see or hear.
03Water

Water — find it, treat itPlan on 3–4 litres per day, more in heat or at altitude. Finding water is half the job; the other half is making it safe.

Finding water
  • Rank your sources: falling rain and snow are safest, then springs, then fast-moving streams, then lakes. Stagnant, foamy, or dead-animal-adjacent water is last resort only.
  • Follow the animals. Birds circling at dawn and dusk, converging game trails, and bees all point toward water.
  • Dig at the outside bend of a dry riverbed, under the shade side of cliffs, and beneath green vegetation — damp sand means a water table close by.
  • Catch it passively: tie a clear plastic bag around a sunlit leafy branch — transpiration yields clean water over hours. Stretch a tarp into a low dish for rain and dew.
  • Wipe dew off rocks and grass with an absorbent cloth before sunrise; wring it into a container.
Clear is not clean. Giardia and other pathogens are invisible. Melting snow? Melt it rather than eating it — eating snow chills your core and speeds dehydration.
Making it safe

The gold standard — kills everything that matters.

  • Bring water to a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 2,000 m / 6,500 ft, where water boils cooler).
  • Boiled water tastes flat because it loses oxygen — shake it hard in a closed bottle to restore taste.
  • This is why your kit needs a metal container, not just plastic bottles.

Removes particles and most bacteria and protozoa — but not all viruses.

  • Commercial filters rated 0.1–0.2 micron handle giardia and most bacteria; pair with chemical treatment where viruses are a risk.
  • Field filter — layered cloth, charcoal, sand, gravel in a cut bottle — improves water, doesn't purify it. Always boil or treat afterwards.
  • Pre-filter cloudy water through cloth first; sediment eats your filter's lifespan.

Lightweight backup that treats what filters miss.

  • Unscented household bleach: 2 drops per litre of clear water (double for cloudy), mix, wait 30 minutes. It should smell faintly of chlorine.
  • Chlorine dioxide tablets are the most effective common option — they also kill viruses and cryptosporidium over a 4-hour wait.
  • Iodine works in 30 minutes but is unsafe for pregnant women and thyroid conditions.

SODIS — solar disinfection. Free, slow, weather-dependent.

  • Fill a clear PET bottle, lay it on a dark surface in full sun for 6 hours — two full days if skies are cloudy.
  • Works only with strong sunlight and water already clear enough to read a newspaper through.
  • For salt water or chemically contaminated water, only distillation (a solar still) makes it drinkable.
04Fire

Fire — heat, light, water, rescueOne fire does five jobs: warmth, purified water, cooked food, a signal, and the morale to keep going. Build it in this order.

  1. 1

    Tinder — the spark catcher

    Dead-dry material finer than a matchstick: birch bark, fatwood shavings, dry grass, feather-sticked twigs, or charred cloth. Carry tinder even when you can find wood — a zip-lock of dryer lint weighs nothing.

  2. 2

    Kindling — the bridge

    Build a ladder of sizes: pencil-lead, pencil, then finger-thick. Dry wood snap-test: it breaks with a crack, not a bend. Gather standing dead branches — anything on damp ground is suspect.

  3. 3

    Fuel — the long burn

    Wrist-thick logs added gradually, not dumped on a young flame. Collect twice the wood you think you need — you will be wrong, and night firewood gathering is how people wander off and get lost.

  4. 4

    Ignition — three ways, always

    Ferrocerium rod (works soaked, 10,000+ strikes), stormproof matches and lighter (keep one on your body, one in the pack), or friction — bow drill with a spindle, hearth board, bearing block, and bowstring from paracord.

  5. 5

    Lay & tend

    Teepee for fast flame, log cabin for a long steady burn, star to ration fuel. Light into the wind, feed from the upwind side, and never leave a fire alive.

Fire site & safety
  • Clear a 3-metre circle to bare earth; check overhead for branches and above for a canopy that traps smoke.
  • Shelter the flame from wind but keep it ventilated — fire breathes. Reflect heat toward your shelter with a log or rock wall.
  • On snow or wet ground, build a platform of green logs so the fire doesn't drown in its own melt.
Extinguishing — drown, stir, feel
  • Drown every ember with water (or smother with soil), stir the ashes, drown again.
  • Feel with the back of your hand — if it's too hot to touch, it's too hot to leave.
  • A fire you abandon can smoulder underground for days. This is how wildfires start.
Practice in the rain before you need it. Fire-making under dry, calm conditions teaches you nothing about the day it matters.
05Food & Foraging

Food — the slow problemYou can live three weeks without food. Never risk illness, energy, or safety chasing calories in the first days. Food is a last-third problem.

Forage smart

Learn a handful of abundant, unmistakable plants for your region — dandelion (all parts), cattail (roots, shoots, pollen), pine (needle tea, inner bark), acorns (boil to leach tannins). Positive ID or pass.

Fish, don't hunt

Hunting burns calories with low odds. Fishing and trapping work while you rest: hooks improvised from pins, bone, or thorns, baited lines checked at dawn and dusk. Trapping needs practice and legal cover — know before you go.

Cook everything

Heat destroys most parasites and many toxins. Insects (avoid hairy, brightly coloured, or foul-smelling ones), fish, and unfamiliar plants are all safer cooked. Boiling keeps the nutrients in the pot — drink the broth.

Universal edibility test — when nothing is familiar

One plant at a time. Nothing else in your stomach. Water available. Each stage: wait 8 hours before the next.

  1. 1

    Skin: crush a piece, hold it against your inner elbow for 8 hours. Burning, rash, or numbness — reject.

  2. 2

    Lips & tongue: touch, hold 15 seconds, wait. Then a tiny taste held in the mouth — bitter, soapy, or numbing, spit and reject.

  3. 3

    Swallow a small piece. Wait 8 hours. Any nausea, cramps, or other symptoms — induce nothing, just drink water and reject the plant.

  4. 4

    Only then eat a normal portion of that one plant. Introduce new plants the same way, one per day at most.

Reject on sight
  • Any plant with milky or discoloured sap
  • Umbrella-shaped flowers — some resemble deadly hemlock
  • Anything with a soapy or bitter taste after cooking
  • Mushrooms of any kind without expert ID — the risk/reward never justifies it
  • White and yellow berries — roughly half are toxic; the "birds eat it" test is a myth, birds tolerate toxins humans can't
Calorie maths: an hour of stumbling after game can burn the calories of a day's food. Sitting still, staying warm, and conserving energy is eating.
06First Aid

First aid — the chapter that saves a life todayEverything here is first-response knowledge for when help is hours away. A wilderness first-aid course is the single best weekend you can spend on this page's advice.

Severe bleeding

1. Firm, direct pressure on the wound with the cleanest cloth available. 2. Soak-through? Add layers on top — never remove the first. 3. Keep pressure; elevate the limb. 4. Tourniquet only for uncontrolled limb bleeding — write down the time it went on.

Not responding

Check: Danger → Response → Airway → Breathing. No normal breathing: start CPR — 30 compressions to 2 breaths, chest compressed 5–6 cm, at 100–120 per minute. Send someone for help and a defibrillator if there is one.

Hypothermia

Shivering, fumbling, slurring = act. Gentle handling (rough movement can stop a cold heart), strip wet layers, insulate from the ground, wrap including head and neck, warm sweet drinks if fully alert. Never rub the limbs or use direct high heat.

Heat stroke

Hot skin, confusion, collapse — this kills fast. Shade, strip, soak with any cool water and fan continuously; ice packs to neck, armpits, groin. Stop fluids if consciousness drops. Cool first, transport second.

Snakebite

Move away from the snake, keep calm and still, remove rings and watches before swelling, immobilise the limb at or below heart level, and carry the person out if possible. Never cut, suck, ice, or tourniquet a bite — and never chase the snake for ID.

Fractures & sprains

Splint as found — immobilise the joint above and below the injury, padded, without trying to straighten it. Check fingers or toes beyond the splint stay warm and pink. Cold, blue, or numb means loosen.

Choking

Can they cough or speak? Encourage coughing. Cannot: 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades, then 5 abdominal thrusts, alternating until the object clears or they collapse — then CPR.

Shock & burns

Shock (pale, cold, clammy, weak): lay flat, raise legs, keep warm, nothing by mouth. Burns: cool running water for 20 minutes, remove jewellery early, cover loosely with clean film. No ice, butter, or creams.

07Navigation

Navigation — know where you areGetting lost is a thinking error before it's a map error. Know north, know your handrails, and know when to stop moving.

With a compass

Set your bearing, hold the compass level, rotate your body until the red needle sits in the orienting arrow, and walk to the direction-of-travel line. Pick a landmark on that line and walk to it — compasses drift, handrails don't.

With a stick — the sun

Stand a stick in flat ground; mark the shadow tip. Wait 15 minutes, mark again. The line between marks runs west → east (first mark is west). Stand with the west mark to your left: you're facing north. Southern hemisphere, reverse it.

With the stars

North: extend the Big Dipper's two pointer stars about five times their gap to reach Polaris. South: lengthen the Southern Cross's long axis 4.5 times and drop straight to the horizon. An analog watch works by day: point the hour hand at the sun — the bisector to 12 is south.

When you realise you're lost
  1. 1

    S.T.O.P. — exactly as chapter 01. Fear walks people in circles; studies show lost travellers drift reliably to the left.

  2. 2

    If people know where you are or you told someone your plan — stay put. A stationary person is a findable person; a signal at a known location beats a track no one can follow.

  3. 3

    If you must move, follow handrails: rivers, ridgelines, power lines, fences. Rivers lead downhill, and downhill eventually leads to people, roads, or the coast.

  4. 4

    Mark your line of travel — stack cairns, break branches upward — so you can backtrack and searchers can read your direction.

Folk signs — use as hints, never as proof
  • Moss grows on the north side — sometimes. Shade, moisture, and wind confuse it.
  • Snow melts on the south face — only in consistent northern-hemisphere sun.
  • Rings on tree stumps are denser on the sunnier side — again, only loosely.

These are suggestions, not navigation. One solid celestial method beats all of them combined.

The best navigation tool is a habit: every trip, tell someone your route and return time, and look backward every few hundred metres so the way home stays familiar.
08Signaling

Signaling — get foundSurvival ends at rescue, and rescue starts with being findable. The universal rule: three of anything means distress.

The signalling toolkit
MethodHow
WhistleThree blasts, pause, repeat. A whistle carries farther than your voice for a fraction of the energy — keep it on your jacket zip, not in your pack.
Signal firePrepare three. Burn green vegetation by day for thick smoke; dry fuel by night for flame. A tire or oil-soaked rag produces a unmistakable black plume.
MirrorAim sunlight with the "V-finger" method — reflect onto your fingertips, then onto the target. Sweep the horizon even with no aircraft in sight; a flash is visible for kilometres.
Ground signalsStomp SOS or an oversized X (need medical help) into snow, sand, or turf — at least 3 m tall, high contrast, in open ground visible from the air.
Cloth & colourBright gear spread in an open clearing, a bright panel on a ridge, or an orange marker held up on a ridge line — contrast and movement catch the eye.
Radio / phoneText before you call — texts queue and send on weak signal. Elevate to high ground for a bar of reception. A PLB or satellite messenger is the gold standard.
SOS — the one message everyone knows

Three short, three long, three short — in sound, light, or ground markings. Then a one-minute pause and repeat. Other codes worth knowing:

  • Three fires in a row or triangle = distress.
  • One blast = "where are you?" · two = "come here" — distress is always three.
  • Arrow of rocks = direction you've travelled, for searchers following your trail.
Signal where aircraft can see it: open ground, high contrast, away from overhangs. The best signal in the world, buried in a forest clearing under canopy, is invisible.
