10.1Cold Physiology

Cold-wet, cold-dry, and the COLD ruleAbove −10 °C you fight meltwater as much as cold; below it, the snow turns dry and the wind becomes the enemy. The manuals divide winter this way because the tactics differ.

COLD — the clothing checklist
  • Keep it Clean. Dirt and oil pack down fibres and destroy the dead-air insulation your clothes actually are.
  • Avoid Overheating. Sweat-soaked clothing is tomorrow's hypothermia — ventilate and shed layers before you sweat, not after; move at a pace you could hold all day.
  • Wear it Loose and in Layers. The warmth is the trapped air between layers, not the fabric — tight clothes compress it away and cut circulation to extremities.
  • Keep it Dry. Wet clothing conducts heat away many times faster than dry. Moisture comes from two directions: outside (snow, rain, slush) and inside (sweat). Brush snow off before it melts; change wet base layers at once.
The four killers, in the manuals' order: wet, wind, cold and heat — and cold-wet (−10 °C and warmer, melting snow) is deadlier than cold-dry precisely because wet clothing robs heat while you still feel "not that cold". Frostbite risk peaks in the messy temperatures.
Dehydration — the cold's hidden accomplice
  • Cold suppresses thirst while heavy clothing makes you sweat more than you think; the frightened, confused lost person dehydrates without noticing, and dehydration directly predisposes hypothermia and frostbite.
  • Never eat snow as water: it takes twelve 8-oz glasses of snow to make one glass of water, and eating it chills your core from the inside. Melt first — over a fire, or in a wide-mouth bottle hung under your outer clothing where body heat does the work.
  • Sip warm water all day in snow country; a transpiration bag on sunlit broadleaf bushes beats every other field water source when supplies run out.
  • When water is short, don't eat: all food requires water to digest.
Carbon monoxide — "the breath of death"
  • More people have died of CO in a running car in a blizzard than have frozen: never sleep with the engine running; open a window 1 inch each side (leeward 2 if snow blows), clear the tailpipe constantly.
  • A V-8 burns ~1 gallon/hour at idle; 15 minutes on, 15 off doubles your comfortable heat time on the same fuel.
  • Engineless heat: a votive or yahrzeit candle in a coffee can burns ~24 hours. Snow caves and sealed shelters need the same ventilation discipline.
10.2Snow Shelters

Building with snowWhere snow is the only material, snow is the shelter. The quinzee procedure below is the Cold Weather Survival manual's, verbatim in its numbers.

The quinzee — a dome for three or four
  1. 1

    Stir the snow in a 6–8 ft quarry area with a stout staff — stirring destroys the crystal structure so the pile will sinter into strength.

  2. 2

    Pile it up around an 8-ft centre marker pole until the mound is 6 ft high and 12 ft across. This is the long, easy part — done slowly to avoid sweating.

  3. 3

    Let it settle one to two hours. This wait is structural, not optional — dig too early and the roof cuts like sugar.

  4. 4

    Insert gauge sticks — 30 or 40 one-foot sticks pushed through the dome's surface, so you leave exactly a 1-ft roof everywhere you dig.

  5. 5

    Hollow it out through a 24-inch entrance, digging to the gauge sticks and rotating diggers to avoid overheating; pile spoil around the door as a windbreak.

  6. 6

    Vent and door: two or three vents (the centre pole marks the crown); a door plug of evergreen boughs cinched with cord around a toggle stick — pull it into the doorway from inside by its cord.

Other snow shelters
  • T-snow cave: dig horizontally into a deep drift at a "T" — the vertical shaft drops cold air out the sleeping level; smooth the ceiling by hand so drip points don't form.
  • Snow block shelter: when snow packs, cut blocks and wall a trench or lean-to roof; block-quarrying needs a snow saw or shovel — carry one above the treeline, always.
  • Tree well: the wells under dense conifers are dug-out ready-made rooms with a canopy roof — the fastest deep-forest night shelter.
  • The pocket shelter: a foil space blanket (56″ × 84″, 4 oz) wrapped foil-side-in over head and shoulders, seated on your pack or fir boughs, clothes-pinned shut — no insulation, but wind and wet are what kill fastest.
Fire or no fire? Many have survived the worst weather sitting out a night by a fire — survival is body-heat maintenance, and a fire also signals rescuers. Snow shelter is for when fuel doesn't exist.
10.3Ice Crossings

Reading ice, from the Canadian arctic manual"Ice is at best a product of poor quality control" — the manual's own words. Blue or green ice with few cracks and no cloudy air bubbles is good ice; everything else is suspect at any thickness.

Where ice lies to you
  • Stream inlets and outlets — current erodes ice from beneath at any thickness.
  • Banks, islands and suspected sandbars — "false ice" frozen to the bottom or bridging air, structurally nothing.
  • Decaying vegetation in bogs and muskeg — plant gas rises into the ice and hollows it.
  • Candle ice — vertical crystal columns that crumble; never trust it if another route exists.
  • Rotten ice — dull, chalky, brittle top or bottom layer with zero carrying capacity.
Measure, don't guess: chop a hole, push a pole through to confirm all layers are penetrated, and measure depth every 30 feet with vehicles — more where ice looks wrong. Multi-layer sheets are far weaker than one solid layer of the same total depth.
10.4Cold Injuries

The cold-injury checklistThe TC 21-3 injury list, condensed: what to watch for in yourself and others, in the order the manual lists them.

Hypothermia

The core cools: watch the "umbles" — stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, grumbles. Shivering that stops while the victim is still cold is a dire sign. Rewarm gently, horizontally, with insulation from the ground; never rub limbs or use high direct heat.

Frostbite

Frozen tissue is numb, waxy, grey or white. Protect from further cold, never rub with snow (a folk treatment the manuals explicitly reject), never thaw over a fire or stove — rewalk-injured tissue refreezes catastrophically. Warm-water rewarming only if refreezing is impossible.

Trench / immersion foot

Hours of damp above freezing: numb, pale, then swollen and red feet. Dry and elevate; the damage can outlast the emergency. This is the cold-wet injury that ruins more marches than frostbite.

Dehydration

Cold masks thirst while layered clothing sweats. Warm sweet drinks at every halt; check urine colour and volume like equipment.

Carbon monoxide

Headache, confusion, nausea inside sealed shelters and running vehicles. Ventilate before symptoms — CO kills sleepers, and it collects at the level you lie at.

Snow blindness & tent eye

UV glare off snow burns the eyes hours before pain arrives — slit goggles or grease-sooted cloth strips are the field preventives; smoke-filled shelters cause the chronic "tent eye" the manual warns about.

Winter movement, in brief
  • Improvised snowshoes: green boughs bound underfoot (spruce works), or a frame of bent green wood laced with cord — without flotation you posthole, burning ten times the energy and soaking your legs.
  • Whiteout: drop to a rope-and-leapfrog method or stop; dead-reckoning by pace count and compass only, or navigate from handrail to handrail.
  • River valleys in winter: travel the high benches, not the valley floors — cold air pools low and the ice over currents is never honest.
  • Snow-camp routine: bough-cutting areas away from camp, marked water points, snow walls — the bivouac discipline of TC 21-3's Chapter 7 is a checklist worth reading before the first snow.
