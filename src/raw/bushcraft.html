Bushcraft & Tools

11.1Rope From Nothing

Cordage from the bushGraves' full rope-making chapter: the three-fibre test, the materials table with breaking strains, and the reverse-twist method that turns any fibrous plant into rope — the same stages, he notes, as a modern rope factory.

Is the material any good? Three tests
  1. 1

    Pull it. Strength first: a straight, steady pull on a length of the material.

  2. 2

    Roll and twist it. Between the fingers — if the rolled fibres hold together without snapping apart, it passes.

  3. 3

    Tie a thumb knot and tighten it. If the material doesn't cut itself and the knot pulls taut — and the fibres "bite" rather than slip — it will make rope. Stiff green material can be passed briefly through hot flame to burst the sap cells and make it pliable.

Spinning cord by hand
  • Gather fibres into loose strands of even thickness, roughly 15–20 fibres per strand at 3 mm or less. Twist each strand clockwise — the twist itself holds the fibres.
  • Lay two, three or four strands together with an anti-clockwise twist while the individual strands keep their clockwise twist — opposing twists lock the rope together. This is the whole secret; strand twist and lay twist must oppose.
  • Keep tension equal on every strand and the lay even — one strand wrapping the others instead of lying beside them is the beginner's tell.
  • One person works alone by rolling fibre along the thigh, feeding new fibre in with the free hand. For serious lengths (50–90 m), set a rope walk: crank spinners, feeders under each arm, and crossbars every 2–3 m so spun strands never touch the ground.
  • Fine cord of under 1 mm, carefully spun, holds 10–15 kg — snare and fishing-line grade.

Double the diameter, quadruple the strength; halve it, quarter it. For safety, Graves says, always assume the lowest figure for a material until you've load-tested your own rope for its actual job.

Harvesting rules
  • Cut, never pull grasses, sedges and rushes — cutting above ground is pruning, pulling is destruction; take from the biggest clumps over a wide area.
  • Inner bark works best from dead or half-dead branches (the gum has dried and teasings out as powder). Only ever cut a branch here and there — never fell a tree for cordage.
  • Palm fibre is free harvest at the leaf/trunk junction and beneath standing palms.
  • Soak, boil, or dry-and-tease materials heavy with vegetable gum before spinning.
11.2Containers & Cooking

Cooking without a potGraves' utensil-free kitchen: a hole in the ground, a piece of bark, and a fire's worth of honest stones.

Hot-stone boiling
  1. 1

    Scoop a shallow bowl in the ground and line it with groundsheet, paper, a shirt, or bark — anything that holds water.

  2. 2

    Heat 20–30 stones, each 5–7.5 cm across, in a quick fire of small sticks until nearly red hot — at least ten minutes. Never take them from a creek bed: waterlogged stones explode in fire.

  3. 3

    Fill the hole with water, then lift the stones in one at a time with fire tongs. The stones will not scorch the cloth or paper; five or six bring a couple of quarts to a boil in two or three minutes.

  4. 4

    Maintain the boil by swapping stones singly — hot in, cold out — indefinitely.

Two more from the same chapter: a kerosene-soaked first-aid bandage is a fire-starter that still serves as a bandage; and a flat stone heated nearly red in the fire, dusted clean, grills meat perfectly.
The coolamin — a bark dish
  • Take a flat piece of bark from a species that doesn't split easily — fig family bark is the classic; test by stripping a small piece from a branch first.
  • Soften the green bark in your hands, fold up the two ends, and pin them with thin sharpened pegs or tie them — a waterproof dish for hot-stone cooking.
  • Sap warning: if the sap is coloured — especially white or whitish — keep it out of your eyes; many saps burn skin or blind temporarily.
  • A forked stick with sharpened prongs is the grill; the coolamin is the pot. Between them, a shirt-lined hole is the stew pot of last resort.
11.3Stone Work

Flintknapping — the short courseFrom the knappers' own tutorial collection: how glass-hard stone actually breaks, and the two ways to remove a flake on purpose.

The physics
  • Conchoidal fracture: fine stone (flint, chert, obsidian, even bottle glass) breaks in a characteristic curved shell — a Hertzian cone radiating from the point of impact. Every knapped edge is an arrangement of these cones and their scars.
  • Below the centerline concept: flakes only detach when force enters below the platform's centerline of mass. Strike or press above it and the stone absorbs the blow. This single idea separates knapping from breaking rocks.
  • Platform preparation: the edge you strike is everything — isolated, ground, and angled so the blow lands below centerline and travels out to a termination. A battered, crushed platform means your angle or force was wrong.
The two techniques
  • Percussion — striking with a soft billet (antler, copper, hard wood; rawhide mallets are the classic) to remove large flakes and thin a preform. Soft hammers bite and shear; hard stone hammers crush platforms.
  • Pressure flaking — pushing flakes off with an antler tine or copper tip, braced against the leg or a pad; for edges, notching, and the final point shape.
  • Safety, non-negotiable: obsidian and flint dust is amorphous silica — knap outdoors, wear a respirator for long sessions, and never in camp near food. Leather pads for the holding hand; flakes are surgical.
  • A beginner's sequence: a thick flake for a blank, edge platform, percussion-thin from both faces, pressure-flake the margin, notch last.
Leather & sinew, in brief
  • Rawhide — dried, dehaired skin — becomes the toughest natural lashings and containers when wet-wrapped and dried in place.
  • Sinew — the tendon along a animal's back and legs — separates into fibres, twists into bowstrings and thread, and self-glues when wetted: the original sewing thread, wrapped and dried under tension.
  • Stitches to know: the running stitch and the whipstitch (over-and-over through the edge — the strongest simple leather seam, especially with sinew through awl holes).
  • A scraped, smoked hide or a bark-tanned one makes sheaths, mocassins, and pack straps; the Foxfire books carry the full processes at length.
Improvising tools
  • Bone: fresh long bones split into blanks — awls, fish hooks, scrapers; bake or boil to harden edges.
  • Stone: any flake with a sharp margin is a knife; a fist-sized cobble with a working edge is a chopper; grind and hone on wet sandstone.
  • Wood: fire-hardened (charring the point of green wood) makes digging sticks and spear points serviceable; charcoal plus sand on hide makes an abrasive.
  • The MacGyver handbook in the library catalogues hundreds more improvised-device patterns from common materials.
11.4Caching

Caching supplies — the Special Forces methodTC 31-29A's planning doctrine, translated to civilian terms: pre-positioning supplies so that future-you can recover them exactly, when it matters.

The seven planning factors
  • Purpose and contents — what goes in decides where it goes: shelf-life items need rotation access; bulky items need isolation.
  • Who might find it — in civilian terms: will construction, new owners, or curious neighbours unearth it? Observe the site and its people before committing.
  • Local activity — normal life (a new building, a cleared trail) is the main discovery risk; imagine the site across seasons.
  • One site, one purpose — don't stack secrets on the same spot; one discovery compromises everything.
  • Packaging — burial demands a genuinely waterproof container: sealed steel or plastic, desiccant inside, and wrapping that survives soil acid and years.
  • Fewest possible people — everyone who knows is a risk vector; emplace and recover alone or in the smallest pair.
  • Method — burial is default (concealment, stable temperature); submersion almost never justifies its recovery difficulty.
Site selection — the three tests
  • Can it be found again from written instructions alone — by someone who has never been there? No distinct permanent landmarks within measurable distance, no cache.
  • Two secure routes in and out — primary and alternate, both with natural concealment.
  • Workable in all seasons — snow holds trails and frozen ground doubles digging time; summer foliage that exposes the site rules it out.
Marking — the final reference point
  • Instructions run general area → immediate reference point → final reference point (FRP) → cache, each step unmistakable ("the only bridge on the named road between X and Y").
  • The FRP must be identifiable, permanent, close enough for precise linear measurement to the cache, and reachable by a simple route description.
  • Measure with metal — a knotted wire line. Cloth tape and twine stretch and shrink when wet; the manual is emphatic.
  • Bury below frost and root action, restore the ground so it photographs as untouched, and record recovery bearings to two landmarks in your own cache notes — stored away from the site.
