02Shelter

Shelter — beat the elementsHypothermia is the number-one outdoor killer, and it starts at +10 °C on a wet, windy day. Shelter is your first build, every time.

Debris hut

The all-rounder with no tools. A ridgepole frame draped with sticks, piled under 60–90 cm of dry leaves. Pile leaves inside too — you want to be the filling, not the tent.

Lean-to

Fastest windbreak and rain roof, built against a ridgepole between two trees. Reflect a fire's heat into the open side — it shelters from weather, not from cold alone.

A-frame

Two poles in an A with ribs along one side. Sheds rain well and blocks wind from one direction. Same rule as all natural shelters: thick debris bedding, small inside volume.

Snow cave

Counter-intuitive but true: snow insulates. Dig into a drift or pile and hollow it, keep the roof rounded, and always poke a fist-sized ventilation hole through — not up-slope.

Heat escapes downward too. The ground pulls heat out of you faster than the air. A 15 cm mattress of leaves, pine boughs, or grass is not optional — it's half the shelter.
Site selection
  • Build small. A shelter just bigger than your body keeps its air warm. Big shelters are cold shelters.
  • Near materials, near (but not on) water. Carry distance costs calories; building 30 m from a stream avoids cold air pooling and insects.
  • Avoid low ground — cold air and water both flow downhill and pool there overnight.
  • Avoid ridgelines and open flats — maximum wind exposure.
  • Look up before lying down. Dead standing trees and leaning branches — "widowmakers" — fall without warning.
  • Check for water courses: a dry wash can become a river in rain you can't yet see or hear.
01Ground Work

Reading the Ground Before You BuildSite selection and natural shelters — the decision that costs nothing and saves everything.

The Marine Corps survival program judges every shelter against six criteria: protection from rain, snow, wind and sun; heat retention; ventilation (critical if you burn fuel inside, for carbon monoxide as much as carbon dioxide); a drying facility for wet clothes; freedom from natural hazards; and stability against severe weather. Daniel Beard put it more simply a century earlier: no shelter is necessary in fair weather, but when the storm comes some shelter is necessary for health and comfort.

Where not to camp

Avoid exposed hilltops, valley floors that pool cold air and drainage, moist ground, avalanche paths, rock-fall zones, and standing dead timber or “widow-maker” limbs that could drop in a storm. Dry washes flash-flood; snow slopes load and slide.

Where to camp

Seek the lee side of terrain elevations — the protected zone extends horizontally up to three times the height of the elevation — plus dense tree groups, terrain depressions, and ground rich in building material: Beard’s rule is to camp where the “mountain goose” (balsam fir) grows, so bedding is at hand.

Tactical siting

When concealment matters, the USMC standard is the acronym BLISS: Blend with surroundings, Low silhouette, Irregular shape, Small, Secluded. Site the shelter with firewood, water, and signaling ground within reach, and plan for the worst-case storm, not the present weather.

Natural shelters — free, but never blindly trusted

  • Half caves: projecting ledges of horizontally seamed bluestone weather into overhangs big enough for a stooping man — likely the first lean-to camp in America, as charcoal of centuries of camp-fires proves. Close the open face with a rock wall or a bark screen set against poles.
  • Caves and rock overhangs: modify by stacking rocks, logs, or branches across open sides. Check for inhabitants — bears, coyotes, lions, rats, snakes — and for old scat and carcasses that carry disease.
  • Hollow logs: dig out and enhance, hanging a poncho, tarp, or parachute over the opening.
  • Fallen trees: the USMC fallen-tree bivouac is excellent because most of the work is done — verify the tree is stable, cut away underside branches to open a hollow, pile insulation on top and sides, and run a small fire outside.
  • Under-tree pockets: the well beneath a dense spruce or fir stays dry and wind-still; wall the sides and sleep on cut boughs.
  • Ventilation: caves may trap smoke and stale air; a fire inside can become unbearable or dangerous.
  • Gas pockets: mountain caves can hold natural gas — test with a flame at the entrance, never by walking deep inside with a torch.
  • Instability: a ledge that looks solid may be a trap waiting to collapse; probe and load-test anything that shelters you.
Exhaustion kills first. The manuals agree on sequence: shelter before comfort, insulation before framework polish. A crude hut finished before dark beats an elegant one abandoned at dusk. Plan for the worst weather you might meet — in a real emergency you do not choose the conditions.
02First Night

The Debris Hut and the Lean-ToTwo foundations of wilderness sheltercraft — one seals you in leaves, one opens to a fire.

Debris hut, step by step

  1. 1

    Choose and clear

    Pick ground loaded with leaves and sticks, free of floods and widow-makers. Rake back forest debris to bare earth — it evicts insects and starts the floor drying.

  2. 2

    Raise the ridge

    At the foot end, pyramid a stack of logs and bury it with dirt to form a mound; lash two poles into an A-frame at the head (jute twine works, cordage is optional — prop a pole against a tree or use a Y-branch instead). Lay the ridgepole from A-frame to mound: high enough that your feet fit under, low enough that the hut hugs your body. Every inch of dead air is air your body heat must warm.

  3. 3

    Rib and seal

    Lay wrist-thick crosspieces from ground to ridgepole like tent ribs, spacing them a hand-span apart. Crawl inside and pack dirt around the base of each rib to kill the wind that creeps underneath.

  4. 4

    Bury it in debris

    Two feet of leaves and forest litter on every side — minimum. “You can have enough debris, but never too much.” When you think you have enough, you are at the halfway point. A full hut is a full day: Paul Scheiter’s build ran 8 a.m. to 6 p.m. with two people.

  5. 5

    Build the door tunnel

    Frame an entrance tunnel at least three feet long so you can pull debris in behind you and plug the opening from the inside. Stuff it with leaves before you settle — one rushed, poorly sealed plug becomes a night-long cold draft.

The lean-to and reflector combo

The lean-to (Beard’s Adirondack camp) is open-fronted and pairs with a fire in a way a debris hut never can. The USMC construction standard for forested terrain:

  • Anchors: two trees 4–12 inches in diameter, spaced so a man can lie between them — or two stout poles driven in.
  • Horizontal support: a ridge pole at least 3–4 inches in diameter, extending 4–6 inches past each tree, lashed about 1 meter off the ground.
  • Stringers: long poles laid from the support to the ground every 1½ feet; a low wall of rocks or logs under their tips lifts the roof, adding headroom and living space.
  • Weave and cover: saplings woven horizontally between stringers, then waterproof and insulate — bark shingled like siding, browse with tips pointing down held by overlay poles, or a poncho/tarp.
  • Thatch rule: start at the eaves and let each course overlap the butts of the row below, exactly like shingling a roof.

Beard’s refinement: set two uprights, lay the crosspiece through their crotches, rest roof poles against it, and floor the enclosure with balsam browse. Two lean-tos can face each other across a shared fire — the double lean-to variant shelters 2–5 people.

The reflector wall — force multiplier

Every open-fronted shelter needs one: a flat rock or a stack of green logs propped behind the fire, bouncing a surprising amount of heat back into the shelter. Heat a lean-to with a slow fire that throws steady heat over hours, not a roaring burst that dies by midnight.

Debris-hut field lessons. Scheiter’s freezing-night critique: exiting mid-night to relieve yourself dumps all stored heat and takes hours to rebuild — carry a bottle or hold it. Too much interior air space means your body heats volume instead of you; pack the inside with more debris, especially under you. And a debris hut that only “works” in mild weather is untested — deliberately sleep it in cold and rain before you trust your life to it.
03Frames & Thatch

Wickiups, Teepees, and Group SheltersScalable frames from the classic literature, sized by crew and season.

Bark teepee

Lash three sticks at the top, spread them as a tripod, lean more poles against them with butts in a circle. Shingle birch bark around it course by course from the bottom, each row overlapping the one below, held by resting poles. Instant, portable, proven.

Wickiup

Beard’s week-camp “dining room”: six uprights — two carrying the ridge-pole, two carrying the eaves — shingled with browse or birch, elm, spruce, or other bark. Its Indian ancestors were matched to locality: form follows material and climate, not fashion.

Beaver-mat hut

Thatch on the ground, not on the wall: lash a pole frame, pad it with a shingled browse bed, lash a duplicate frame over the top, then erect the finished panel. Compact enough to be practically waterproof, and no large tree is cut — an underbrush-only shelter.

Fagot shack

For country without big timber: bind willow, cane, or alder twigs into equal bundles and lay them like stones, breaking joints, staked in place, with leafy browse as mortar. Plaster inside and out with soft mud and it becomes a permanent adobe shack.

Sapling hoop shelter

The USMC evasion shelter: two parallel rows of saplings 1½–2 feet apart along a 4-foot length, bent over and tied into hoops, covered with water-repellent material, then insulated with leaves, brush, snow, or boughs. One end closed permanently, a hung cloth door at the other.

Poncho shelter

The first shelter to consider for a short stay: fold the sheet in half to find the center, suspend the two center points with cord, stake the four corners with sticks or rocks. Minutes to build, negligible cost.

Sizing a thatched hut for the party

The Ten Bushcraft Books scale the design by occupancy, climate, and time — organize labor so no hands are idle (two cutting poles, one carrying, one stripping lashings, the rest stacking thatch) and stockpile every material before raising a single pole.

If the ridge runs past 10–12 feet, add another fork midway. Use dry, dead timber — lighter to carry and easier on the bush.
04Snow Country

Improvised Snow SheltersThree ways to sleep warm when the only building material falls from the sky.

FM 5-103’s cold-region doctrine: the three basic materials are snow, ice, and frozen soil, and snow behavior decides your method. Dry snow packs poorly; snow compacted by wind, artificially, or after a brief thaw densifies and hardens within hours even at very low temperatures. A uniform cover of at least 10 inches suffices for shelter construction. Cut uniform blocks — typically 8 × 12 × 16 inches, by shovel, machete, or carpenter’s saw — and build block-over-seam in overlapping courses, caulking every seam with loose snow for a snug, draft-free structure. Igloos built this way have survived a whole winter. A mild thaw of 1–2 degrees above freezing is the friendliest working temperature: snow conglomerates and holds any shape.

Snow cave / igloo block hut

Dug or blocked from consolidated drift. Best where wind-packed or thaw-hardened snow lies deep. Dome shape, sleeping platform carved above the entrance cold-pit, puncture a ventilation hole through the roof and never seal it. The block igloo suits deep uniform cover with a cutting tool.

Quinzee (snow-mound hut)

Where snow is too shallow or dry to cut: heap and trample a mound over a pack or upright stick, let it sinter and harden for an hour or more, then hollow it out from the side, keeping walls at least a foot thick. Quinzee snow needs no natural pack — you manufacture it.

Snow trench and thermal frames

Fastest expedient in the manuals: dig a trench just wider than your body, roof it with boughs, poles, or a poncho, then heap snow on top — snow is an insulator as well as a wall. The USMC A-frame takes the same treatment: boughs or tentage woven on the frame, then snow packed over the outside.

  • Wet rocks near fire: heat acting on dampness in sandstone, shale, and stream stones can make them explode — dry them at the fire’s edge first.
  • Carbon monoxide: every enclosed snow shelter with a stove or candle needs a ventilation hole and a patched-but-breathable door; CO accumulates silently in still, sealed domes.
  • Avalanche and corniche: never site a snow shelter beneath loaded slopes; dig in the lee of stable ground, away from the runout.
Work slow, sweat never. Pace snow construction so clothing stays dry, and stamp your platform and paths firm before they freeze at night — what you trample today is tomorrow’s floor.
05Campcraft & Cabin

Beds, Furniture, and the Long-Term CampFrom a shingled bough bed to a notched-log cabin — the axe work that turns a shelter into a home.

Bedding: defeat the ground first

Cold kills from below: the earth pulls heat from a sleeper faster than the night air does. Beard’s balsam bed is the classic answer. Gather twigs in graduated lengths — 18-inch for the foundation, 10–12-inch for the top layer — snapped by hand, not chopped (boughs slap back at a knife), and strung on a 4–5 foot forked carrying stick like fish on a line. Lay the mattress from the head down, shingled so all tips point toward the pillow and each layer overlaps the butts below, until the couch is thick enough to sleep as well as home. Do not economize — thin boughs mean aching bones. Without conifers, substitute springy green branches under dry leaves, hay, or straw, with green material only under a waterproof ground sheet.

Camp furniture in an afternoon

  • Camp table: two forked stakes 3 inches thick and 4–5 feet long, driven until the crotch sits 3 feet above ground and 4–7 feet apart; four side poles nested in the forks; table poles lashed about 2 feet up; seat poles 3 inches thick lashed 15 inches above ground; laced sticks — eight per foot — form the top, and loose seat poles slide in or out to adjust distance.
  • Camp chair: two forked sticks 4 feet long and 3 inches thick with prongs 9–10 and 12–15 inches, a third fork to bind them, and 1-inch sticks lashed close for the seat and 2–3 inches apart up the back. Fifteen minutes of work for hours of comfort.
  • Cooking tripod: interlocking forked sticks with a cross stick, or lashed poles in a plain tripod; sliding the pot along the cross stick varies its height over the flames.
  • Swinging bunk: in swampy or snake-infested country, a 3 × 7 foot lashed pole rectangle slung on ropes — a bed that clears damp and crawlers.

The one-pen log cabin

Beard’s progression ends at the American log cabin — a house that lasts, which shacks with bark-on logs never do.

  • Peel every log. Bark holds moisture, invites decay fungi, and shelters boring insects whose holes admit rain. Lincoln’s cabin survives because its logs were peeled.
  • Snake logs to a skidway — poles resting on a big log — so each timber can be rolled to the walls as needed. Save the largest, straightest logs for the sills; paint mudsill logs with two or three coats of warm creosote to postpone rot indefinitely.
  • Notch the corners: the simple rounded U-notch locks fastest; flat notches, the General Putnam flattened joint, the wedge-and-triangle Pike notch, and northern dovetails each lock tighter at the cost of more axe time.
  • Build the pen solid as if it had no openings; saw door, window, and fireplace openings only after cleats are tacked across to hold the cut log-ends, then frame them permanently. Keep notches evenly spaced so walls rise true even though logs overrun the corners by 2–3 feet; trim the ends flush with a two-handed saw at the end.
  • Chink and mud: drive quartered timber into the cracks between logs, held by spikes, then mud the inside with clay or lime mortar. A timber floor over half-foot joists finishes a ten-foot-wide room.
  • Model first: Beard’s standing advice — build a 15 by 10 inch miniature of one-inch sticks to plan rooms, fireplace, and openings before cutting a single tree.

Axe work safety

  • Never swing a loose-headed axe — only a chump uses a tool whose head can fly off the handle; sight the edge in line with the handle’s butt before trusting it.
  • Clear your swing of brush and overhanging branches that can deflect the blade, and never use a good axe on roots or ground litter where stones ruin the edge.
  • Never stand behind a falling tree: branches snagging neighbors can kick the butt back, and woodsmen have died from the kick. Fell with the wind, notch the fall-side kerf half through, then cut the opposite side higher and step aside as the top wavers.
  • Store it stuck upright in a stump, never on the ground for feet to find.
