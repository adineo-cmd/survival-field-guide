CHAPTER 08 — NAVIGATION

Navigation & Signaling

Getting lost is a thinking error before it is a map error. Map and compass craft, the sun and star methods, terrain association — and the full signalling toolkit that turns survival into rescue.

08THREE OF ANYTHING
MEANS DISTRESS
07Navigation

Navigation — know where you areGetting lost is a thinking error before it's a map error. Know north, know your handrails, and know when to stop moving.

With a compass

Set your bearing, hold the compass level, rotate your body until the red needle sits in the orienting arrow, and walk to the direction-of-travel line. Pick a landmark on that line and walk to it — compasses drift, handrails don't.

With a stick — the sun

Stand a stick in flat ground; mark the shadow tip. Wait 15 minutes, mark again. The line between marks runs west → east (first mark is west). Stand with the west mark to your left: you're facing north. Southern hemisphere, reverse it.

With the stars

North: extend the Big Dipper's two pointer stars about five times their gap to reach Polaris. South: lengthen the Southern Cross's long axis 4.5 times and drop straight to the horizon. An analog watch works by day: point the hour hand at the sun — the bisector to 12 is south.

When you realise you're lost
  1. 1

    S.T.O.P. — exactly as chapter 01. Fear walks people in circles; studies show lost travellers drift reliably to the left.

  2. 2

    If people know where you are or you told someone your plan — stay put. A stationary person is a findable person; a signal at a known location beats a track no one can follow.

  3. 3

    If you must move, follow handrails: rivers, ridgelines, power lines, fences. Rivers lead downhill, and downhill eventually leads to people, roads, or the coast.

  4. 4

    Mark your line of travel — stack cairns, break branches upward — so you can backtrack and searchers can read your direction.

Folk signs — use as hints, never as proof
  • Moss grows on the north side — sometimes. Shade, moisture, and wind confuse it.
  • Snow melts on the south face — only in consistent northern-hemisphere sun.
  • Rings on tree stumps are denser on the sunnier side — again, only loosely.

These are suggestions, not navigation. One solid celestial method beats all of them combined.

The best navigation tool is a habit: every trip, tell someone your route and return time, and look backward every few hundred metres so the way home stays familiar.
08.4Map & Compass

Map and compass, the Army wayFrom GTA 05-02-013, the soldier's pocket map-reading course: coordinates, distance by pacing, azimuths, and the G-M angle that reconciles map with compass.

Coordinates — never be lost, by definition
  • Military maps are overlaid with a numbered grid; a position is read right, then up: halfway between vertical lines 11 and 12 gives "115"; two-tenths between horizontal lines 81 and 82 gives "812". Your six-digit coordinate: 115812.
  • "If you always know your coordinates, you can never be lost" — you may be unable to reach where you're going, but you always know where you are.
  • A clear-plastic coordinate scale and protractor reads the position inside a grid square exactly and measures azimuths; improvised, the edge of a radio antenna or a ration-box side does the straightedge work.
Distance — the pacing method
  • The average person takes 116 paces per 100 m on level ground — and about 148 cross-country, where slopes steal distance. Calibrate your own numbers over a known 600 m before trusting them.
  • Count with pebbles: ten pebbles in the right pocket; move one left for every 100 m walked. Empty right pocket = 1 kilometre. (Knots in a string work too.)
  • Map distance: tick the map distance on a paper edge, hold against the bar scale printed on the map. Around curves, pivot the paper at each bend, marking as you go.
  • At night, untrained walkers circle — pace counts are only straight if the compass governs the line.
Azimuths — direction as a number
  • Direction clockwise from north: east 90°, south 180°, west 270°, north 360°. Plot it by drawing the line and reading a protractor aligned to the grid.
  • Back azimuth (to return): subtract 180° if the azimuth is over 180°, add it if under. 223° back = 43°. A sign error here is the classic way soldiers walk away from their unit at night.
  • Three norths: grid north (map lines), magnetic north (the needle), true north (the Pole). The map's declination diagram gives the G-M angle between the first two, with the conversion rule printed beside it — add or subtract as the diagram instructs, and in high-declination regions failing to convert turns a perfect compass walk into a parallel error of hundreds of metres per kilometre.
  • Compass discipline: the needle loves metal — rifles, vehicles, helmets, power lines. Step clear before shooting an azimuth.
Two ways to hold a lensatic compass
  • Center-hold (faster, works at night): cover folded flat in line with the base, thumbs through the loop, elbows locked into your sides, compass at chin height — turn your whole body toward the object and read under the index line.
  • Compass-to-cheek (most precise): cover vertical, rear sight folded slightly forward, sight the wire on the object through the slot, then glance down the lens for the azimuth.
  • Move object to object: shoot an azimuth to a distinct steering mark, walk to it, repeat. Stopping occasionally to re-shoot the azimuth is what keeps the line straight.
08.5No Compass

Finding direction without a compassThe GTA's field-expedient methods, in its own order of accuracy: shadow-tip first, watch second, stars third — plus the reasons "the sun rises in the east" is only almost true.

Shadow-tip method

Plant a stick in level ground and mark the shadow tip — first mark is always west. Wait 10–15 minutes; the tip moves a few inches; mark it again — second mark is always east. A line through both is your west–east axis; stand with the west mark to your left and you face north, east right, south behind. Quick, easy, very accurate, either hemisphere.

Watch method

Northern temperate zone: point the hour hand at the sun; south lies midway between it and 12 o'clock (13:00 on daylight saving). Southern temperate: point the 12 at the sun; north is midway to the hour hand. If unsure which end is north, remember the sun sits east before noon, west after.

Star method — Polaris

Find the Big Dipper; the last two stars of its cup point directly at the North Star, about five times their separation. Face Polaris and you face due north, east on your right. The manuals' honest caveat: "the sun rises in the east" is a pretty good rule that is seldom exactly right — sunrise point shifts with season and latitude.

Finding your position on the mapOrient the map first — north on paper matching north on the ground — then one known feature is a line, two are a point.

Resection — you have no idea where you are
  1. 1

    Orient the map (compass is best) and don't move it again.

  2. 2

    Pick a feature you can see and find on the map — a water tower, a distinct hilltop. Lay a straightedge against its map symbol and pivot until the edge points at the real feature; draw the line.

  3. 3

    Repeat with a second feature. Where the lines cross is where you stand. A third line is the accuracy check — with good work all three cross in one spot.

Modified resection — you know the line you're on

On a road, riverbank, ridge or trail, your position is somewhere along a known line — one sighting pins it.

  • Orient the map, sight a single known feature as above, and draw the line; where it crosses your linear feature is your position.
  • The manual's improvisations for the straightedge: a rifle cleaning rod, a radio antenna section, the edge of a ration box.
  • Intersection is resection inverted — sighting two known points from an unknown target locates the target on the map; the forward observer's method, and equally good for plotting a distant wildfire or a fallen aircraft.
Terrain association is the skill under it all: contour shapes read as hills, saddles, draws and spurs; matching the country you see to the paper in your hand is faster than any construction — the constructions exist to check it.
